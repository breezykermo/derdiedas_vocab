finished Kapitel 14 ("Geld") and have studied every earlier chapter.

Format: a 15-20 minute conversation between two hosts:
- EXPERT: a fluent, near-native German speaker. Patient, warm, precise.
- LEARNER: a student at exactly this point in the course (end of Kapitel 14). Keen and
  chatty, but makes the kinds of mistakes real learners make at this stage.
The episode is a real conversation about the chapter's topics, not a lecture. The
LEARNER keeps trying to say things in German; the EXPERT keeps the conversation going
and corrects the LEARNER's German as they go.

How the corrections work (the core of the episode):
- The LEARNER makes roughly 12-20 mistakes over the episode. Most should target the
  Kapitel 14 grammar points below; the rest should come from earlier chapters (wrong
  article/gender, verb endings, plural forms, word order, a vocabulary mix-up).
  Mistakes must be plausible for this level, never random nonsense.
- Every mistake gets corrected — none slip past. The EXPERT either recasts it ("Ah, du
  meinst: ...") or stops briefly to explain the rule in one or two sentences, then gives
  a correct model sentence.
- The LEARNER then repeats the corrected sentence and, where it fits, makes a new
  sentence using the same rule correctly, so the listener hears the right form at least
  twice for every time they hear the wrong one.
- Make the mistake and its correction unmistakable to a listener: the EXPERT should say
  clearly what was wrong (e.g. "Nicht 'der Stadt' — 'die Stadt'. Stadt ist feminin.").
- Now and then the EXPERT asks the listener directly: "Hast du den Fehler gehört?
  Mach eine Pause und korrigiere ihn!" — then gives the answer.
- Corrections are kind and encouraging; the LEARNER sometimes self-corrects or
  asks "Warum?" to prompt an explanation.

Language — IMMERSION: the episode is in German. This is for listening comprehension,
so the listener should hear as much German as possible.
- Speak only German. No English at all.
- Make meaning clear IN GERMAN: slow down, repeat, rephrase with known words, give
  examples and contrasts, and have the LEARNER check understanding in German
  ("Was bedeutet ...?", "Also ... heißt ...?").
- Explain grammar in simple German by SHOWING: contrast the wrong and the right form,
  then give several more correct examples. Grammar terms that are close to English are
  fine: Nominativ, Akkusativ, Dativ, Genitiv, maskulin, feminin, neutral, Singular,
  Plural, Artikel, Verb, Endung, Präsens, Perfekt, Präteritum, Konjunktiv, Passiv.
- Never translate whole sentences into English and never switch to English for a whole
  explanation.
- The grammar explanations and glosses further down this source are in English for
  YOUR reference only. Don't read or quote them; teach those points in German.

Hard constraints:
- German vocabulary: use ONLY words from the vocabulary lists in this source (this
  chapter's list plus the earlier-chapter list), along with names, numbers, obvious
  international cognates (Internet, Party, Computer ...) and the small function words
  (articles, pronouns, und, oder, aber, in, auf ...). If you need a word that is not
  listed, explain it once in simple German (or with a one-word English gloss if the
  German explanation would be too hard).
- German grammar: correct sentences use ONLY structures from Kapitel 1-14 (see the
  grammar sections below). Don't introduce tenses, cases or constructions taught later
  in the course, and don't "correct" the LEARNER into a structure they haven't learned.
- Always say nouns with their article (der/die/das) the first time they come up, and
  give the plural when it is listed.

Suggested shape:
1. Quick welcome: the EXPERT introduces the LEARNER and the chapter theme "Geld"
   (Themen listed below).

   talks about their own life and opinions; mistakes and corrections happen naturally.

5. Wrap-up: the LEARNER sums up what they learned — the trickiest corrections of the

--- Thema 14.1: Weimarer Hyperinflation ---

## A. Relative clauses
A. Relative clauses A.1 Relative clauses as modifiers Both English and German have similar ways of adding information to, or “modifying,” nouns. Some kinds of modifiers can simply be tacked on before or after the noun they modify: an adjective: hohe Inflation a noun phrase: die Inflation der 1920er Jahre a prepositional phrase: die Inflation in Deutschland Modifying information that includes a verb, however, requires a relative clause: … die Inflation, die Deutschland in den 1920er Jahren erlebte … the inflation that Germany experienced in the 1920’s Relative clauses in German express the same kind of information as in English, but their formation differs in several important ways: Since a relative clause is a dependent clause, the conjugated verb ( V 1 ) appears at the end of the clause. Clauses in German are normally separated by commas. This means that a comma must be inserted between the noun being modified (often referred to as the antecedent ) and the relative clause that follows it, with a second comma required at the end of the relative clause if the main clause continues after it: … die Inflation, die Deutschland in den 1920er Jahren erlebte, hatte wichtige Konsequenzen für die Politik danach. … the inflation that Germany experienced in the 1920’s had important consequences for the politics afterwards. English has five relative pronouns ( which, that, who, whom and whose ), and the choice of which one to use depends on a variety of subjective and objective factors (How formal do I want to sound? Am I referring to a person or a thing? Is this a restrictive or a non-restrictive clause?). German is actually much more straightforward here, since the choice of the relative pronoun in German depends entirely on two objective factors: (1) the gender and number of the antecedent; and (2) the grammatical function of the relative pronoun within the relative clause. A.1
A.2 Relative pronouns: Nominative Completing the task in Übung 14.2 is virtually identical to producing the most common type of relative clause in German, in which the relative pronoun serves as the subject of the clause and follows immediately after the antecedent, using one of these forms: masculine feminine neuter plural Nominative der die das die You can think of the relative pronoun as a proxy for its antecedent in the clause preceding it. And when the relative pronoun is the subject of the relative clause, it’s identical to the nominative article of the antecedent: 1. … der Chef, der den Betrieb führt [ = der Chef führt den Betrieb] 2. … die Firma, die es herstellt [ = die Firma stellt es her] 3. … das Produkt, das so bekannt ist [ = das Produkt ist so bekannt] 4. … die Leute, die im Betrieb arbeiten [ = die Leute arbeiten im Betrieb] A few more examples: 5. … mit dem Stoff, der für dieses Produkt notwendig ist 6. … wegen der Frau, die das Unternehmen gegründet hat 7. … hat ein Fach studiert, das an vielen Unis angeboten wird 8. … will in Märkten arbeiten, die in Europa entstanden sind Notice in this second set of sentences that the case of the antecedent has no effect whatsoever on the case of the relative pronoun; that is determined solely by the function of the relative pronoun in its own clause. In all of the examples above, the relative pronoun is the grammatical subject of the relative clause — and therefore uses the nominative case. Notice too that while relative clauses usually appear immediately after the antecedent (to make the connection as clear as possible), a V 2 element such as a past participle or a modal infinitive can intervene, as in examples #7 and #8. A.2

## B. Relative clauses: Accusative
B. Relative clauses: Accusative B.1 A relative pronoun can also function as the direct object within the relative clause: … a book that I just read makes the argument that … … the people that we met at the party were really fun. Sentences like these illustrate several differences between the ways English and German use relative pronouns: In both sentences, you can drop the relative pronoun and hardly notice its absence: … a book I just read … … the people we met at the party … English allows this with object relative pronouns, but not with subject relative pronouns. In German, however, the relative pronoun is always required. In the case of the second example above, an English speaker who wants to retain the relative pronoun has multiple options, depending on the level of formality they wish to convey: … the people that we met … … the people who we met … … the people whom we met … (Grammarians who insist that the third option is the only “correct” one are ignoring the realities of sociolinguistic variation.) In German, however, there is only one option, and it depends entirely on the antecedent: … die Leute, die wir getroffen haben … (plural antecedent; accusative case within the clause → die ) B.1
B.2 Relative pronouns that function as direct objects (and therefore require the accusative case) are identical to the accusative definite articles: masculine feminine neuter plural Nominative der die das die Accusative den die das die A few examples: … der Arbeitsplatz, den er wollte … the job (that) he wanted … die Erklärung, die sie mir gegeben hat … the explanation (that) she gave me … das Projekt, das wir entwickelten … the project (that) we developed … die Mitarbeiter, die du kennst … the co-workers (that) you know Here too, the grammatical case of the relative pronoun is dictated by its function in the relative clause, not by the case of the antecedent in the preceding clause. In the following sentences, the case of the antecedent differs from the examples above — but notice how the relative clause remains the same: … für einen Arbeitsplatz, den er wollte … trotz der Erklärung, die sie mir gegeben hat … mit dem Projekt, das wir entwickelten … wegen der Mitarbeiter, die du kennst B.2

## C. Relative clauses: Dative
C. Relative clauses: Dative C.1 When relative pronouns take the dative case, they use the following forms: masculine feminine neuter plural Nominative der die das die Accusative den die das die Dative dem der dem denen As you can see, the masculine, feminine and neuter relative pronouns are identical with the corresponding definite pronouns — with one exception: denen . A few examples: … der Mann, dem sie den neuen Prozess erklärte … the man to whom she explained the new process … the man whom she explained the new process to … the man who she explained the new process to … the man she explained the new process to … die Freundin, der ich von meinem Wochenende erzählte … the friend to whom I talked about my weekend [etc.] … das Kind, dem ich helfen wollte … the child whom I wanted to help [etc.] … diejenigen, denen wir das Produkt zeigten … those to whom we showed the product [etc.] The glosses are a reminder that English allows for a variety of relative pronoun choices, depending on the verb in play and the level of formality desired — including the choice to drop the relative pronoun altogether. German has only one option: The relative pronoun appears at the beginning of the clause, followed by the subject and whatever other information is included, with V 1 at the end. The dative case functions in relative clauses just as it does in independent clauses, namely, to identify: the object of a dative verb ( helfen, antworten, glauben, …); an indirect object (the recipient of the direct object); the object of a dative preposition ( aus, bei, mit, …); the object of a variable preposition ( an, auf, vor, …) when the context denotes position rather than motion. The exercises and discussion in this section focus on the first two of these functions: Objects of dative verbs ( C.2 ), and indirect objects ( C.3 ). The use of relative pronouns as objects of prepositions will be explained in Thema 4 . C.1
C.2 Dative relative pronouns: Dative verbs A relative pronoun appears in the dative case when it functions in its clause as the object of a dative verb. Fast alle Migranten, denen wir helfen wollen, … Almost all immigrants who(m) we want to help … The core vocabulary up to this point includes the following dative verbs: antworten to answer, to give an answer (to) dienen to serve drohen to threaten entsprechen to correspond (to) gefallen to be pleasing (to) gehören to belong (to) gelingen to succeed, be successful glauben to believe [a person] helfen to help In addition, German has idiomatic phrases that require dative objects: gut / schlecht gehen It’s going well / poorly (for) egal sein It doesn’t matter (to) By now you’re familiar with how these work in independent clauses: Dieser Prozess dient einer bestimmten Funktion … Es geht meinem Bruder nicht so gut . Diese Probleme drohen dem wirtschaftlichen Wachstum … Ist es diesen Menschen wirklich egal , ob …? Die Professorin antwortete der Studentin so: … Das Auto gehört seinen Eltern ... These sentences can be reformulated with relative clauses, so that the verb/object information modifies a noun: Die Funktion, der dieser Prozess dient , … The function that this process serves … Mein Bruder, dem es nicht gut geht , … My brother, who is not doing well, … Das wirtschaftliche Wachstum, dem diese Probleme drohen , … The economic growth that these problems are threatening, … Menschen, denen diese Situation egal ist , … People for whom this situation doesn’t matter … Die Studentin, der die Professorin geantwortet hat, … The student, whom the professor answered in this way, … Seine Eltern, denen das Auto gehört , … His parents, to whom the car belongs, … C.2
C.3 Dative relative pronouns: Indirect objects A relative pronoun appears in the dative case when it functions in its clause as the indirect object. Two of the sentences you translated in Übung 14.9 contain relative clauses with this construction: Das Kind, dem der Vater ein Eis° gegeben hat, … Ich rief einen Freund an, dem ich die Mathe-Aufgabe zeigen wollte, … Relative clauses such as these are used with verbs such as geben, zeigen and sagen that can take both a direct object ( what is given/shown/said) as well as an indirect object ( to whom it is given/shown/said). Other verbs in this category include: empfehlen erzählen holen kaufen verkaufen öffnen zeigen Der Freund, dem ich den Film empfohlen habe, … The friend to whom I recommended the film … Die meisten Besucher, denen man den Campus zeigt, denken … Most visitors to whom one shows the campus think … Die Großmutter, der das Mädchen Kuchen und Wein bringen sollte, … The grandmother, to whom the girl was supposed to bring cake and wine, … C.3

## D. hin | her
D. hin | her German is precise about spatial relationships. You’ve seen this in the Dative/Accusative distinction in variable prepositions (in a location: auf dem Tisch ; moving toward a location: auf den Tisch ). And you may have noticed it in the way German has multiple words for where , once again dependent on location ( wo ) as opposed to movement toward a location ( wohin ) or from a location ( woher ). This section explains the logic behind the use of hin and her — words that you’ve seen in one form or another beginning in the very first chapter: Wo her kommst du? — and how they are used in compound formations. D.1 German uses hin in conjunction with other words to add or emphasize a spatial/directional perspective: moving away from the speaker toward somewhere else. hin gehen to go there (i.e., somewhere other than the speaker's location) hin auf upwards (i.e., up and away from where the speaker is) hin ein inside (from the perspective of someone outside) The word her means just the opposite: toward the speaker from somewhere else. her kommen to come from (i.e., from a location other than the speaker's) her auf upwards (i.e., from somewhere below the speaker) her ein inside (from the perspective of someone already inside) When hin and her are used with verbs (as in hingehen and herkommen ), they function as separable prefixes: Alle meine Freunde gehen zum Konzert, und ich will auch hin gehen. When used in questions, hin and her can function like separable prefixes, or they can attach to wo- , forming one word: Wo her kommst du? / Wo kommst du her ? Er hat gefragt, wo her du kommst. / Er hat gefragt, wo du her kommst. Wo hin gehst du? / Wo gehst du hin ? Sie will wissen, wo hin du gehst. / Sie will wissen, wo du hin gehst. Hin and her combine to form the idiom back and forth: Sie muss jede Woche zwischen München und Frankfurt hin und her fahren. Every week she has to drive back and forth between Munich and Frankfurt. D.1
D.2 Hin- / her- + Preposition + Verb The previous section shows examples of hin and her combining with prepositions, and with verbs such as gehen and kommen . Sometimes all three elements ( hin-/her- + preposition + verb) are in play: Das kleine Mädchen hat zum Baum hinaufgeschaut … The little girl looked up at the tree … Wir kamen spät an, also mussten wir ins Kino hineinlaufen .* We arrived late, so we had to run into the movie theater. * Ein- is frequently used here in place of the preposition in . These are the most frequently used combinations of hin-/her- with prepositions (including the colloquial shortening of her- to r- ): hinein- into (from an outside perspective) hinaus- outside (from an inside perspective) hinauf- upwards (from a lower perspective) hinunter- downwards (from a higher perspective) herein- / rein- come into (from the perspective of someone inside) heraus- / raus- come out of (from the perspective of outside) herauf- / rauf- come up (to where the speaker already is) Notice how these phrases underscore the spatial perspectives in the following cartoon: In combinations like these, rein and runter are special cases. Their use conforms to the rule whenever they are connected with kommen, as in an invitation to Come on in or Come on down! (i.e., toward the speaker). But they are routinely used as well in colloquial speech with gehen to express motion away from the speaker, as in I’m going in / I’m going down. Compare: Bitte, kommen Sie rein ! Please, come on in! (i.e., toward the speaker) But: Also, ich gehe jetzt rein , egal was der Türsteher sagt. All right, I’m going in now, regardless of what the bouncer says. Der Wein ist im Keller. Ich geh’ mal runter und hol’ eine Flasche. The wine is in the cellar. I’ll go down and get a bottle. D.2

## E. Relative clauses: Genitive
E. Relative clauses: Genitive Genitive relative pronouns function very similarly in English and German: … ein Kunde, dessen Geld auf einem Schweizer Bankkonto liegt … a customer whose money is in a Swiss bank account … eine Kundin, deren Wertsachen in einem Tresorfach liegen … a customer whose valuables are in a safety deposit box They cannot be dropped in either language; and in both languages, they indicate a relationship of possession between the antecedent and whatever follows the relative pronoun. In other words, whenever whose appears in a statement (as distinct from questions beginning with Whose …?) , a genitive relative pronoun is required in German. Their forms are similar (but not identical) to the corresponding genitive definite articles: masculine feminine neuter plural Genitive dessen deren dessen deren Be careful: It is the antecedent of the relative pronoun — and not the word following the relative pronoun — that determines the gender of the pronoun itself. … der Mitarbeiter, dessen Tochter ein Konto auf der Bank hat … the (male) worker whose daughter has an account at the bank … die Chefin, deren Auto vor der Tür wartet … the (female) boss whose car is waiting at the door Mitarbeiter is masculine, hence dessen — even though it’s connected with Tochter, which is feminine. Chefin is feminine, hence deren — even though Auto is neuter. E

## F. Relative clauses: Prepositions
F. Relative clauses: Prepositions F.1 A relative pronoun can serve as the object of a preposition within its own clause, in both English and German: … die Kreditkarte, mit der ich zahlen möchte … the credit card with which I’d like to pay … die Professorin, für die ich eine Arbeit schreibe ... the professor for whom I’m writing a research paper … Kurse, in denen man viel lernen kann … courses in which one can learn a lot The rules for forming such sentences are relatively simple: The prepositional phrase (i.e., the preposition and the relative pronoun object) comes at the very beginning of the clause, with the preposition first, followed immediately by the relative pronoun. The grammatical subject of the relative clause and any other elements (direct objects, indirect objects, adverbial elements) come after this prepositional phrase. V 1 appears at the end of the clause. Since genitive prepositions are very rarely used in relative clauses like this, your choice will be between accusative and dative relative pronouns: masculine feminine neuter plural Accusative den die das die Dative dem der dem denen Learning these rules is the easy part. The challenge comes in applying them consistently in contexts in which most English speakers would hardly ever say anything as formal as the credit card with which I’d like to pay or courses in which one can learn a lot . In most conversational contexts, you are more likely to say (using the examples above): the credit card I’d like to pay with (or) the credit card that I’d like to pay with courses you can learn a lot in (or) courses that you can learn a lot in English allows you to delete the object altogether and let the preposition dangle on its own. If the object appears, English allows it to stand by itself, with the preposition elsewhere in the clause. But neither of these options is acceptable in German. Regardless of the level of formality, the preposition and relative pronoun must appear at the beginning of the relative clause, with the conjugated verb at the end. This results in a level of difficulty that causes many English speakers to avoid this construction. F.1
F.2 The next step is to incorporate fixed combinations into this process: arbeiten + an [Dat.] bestehen + aus bitten + um denken + an [Acc.] erzählen + von es geht + um handeln + von lachen + über [Acc.] leiden + an [Dat.] schreiben + über [Acc.] schreiben + an [Acc.] sprechen + über [Acc.] warten + auf [Acc.] A few examples: … die Geschichte, über die wir gelacht haben … the story (that) we laughed about … zwei Freunde, auf die er wartet … two friends (who/whom) he’s waiting for … ein Projekt, an dem sie jetzt arbeitet … a project (that) she’s working on now … Faktoren, an die man denken muss … factors (that) one has to think about … das Geld, um das du gebeten hast … the money (that) you asked for … die Veränderungen, um die es geht … the changes (that are) at stake … der Club, von dem sie uns erzählt haben … the club (that) they told us about The same applies to fixed combinations that include reflexive pronouns: sich erinnern + an [Acc.] sich beschäftigen + mit sich freuen + auf [Acc.] sich freuen + über [Acc.] sich verlassen + auf [Acc.] Some examples using these: … ein Fehler, an den ich mich erinnerte … a mistake (that) I remembered … das Risiko, mit dem die Firma sich beschäftigen muss … the risk (that) the company has to deal with … ein Treffen, auf das sie sich vorbereiten muss … a meeting (that) she has to get ready for … ein Urlaub, auf den wir uns alle freuen … a vacation (that) we’re all looking forward to … Freunde, auf die man sich verlassen kann … friends (that/who[m]) you can count on F.2

--- Thema 14.4: Work-Life-Balance ---

## G. Relative pronouns: was
G. Relative pronouns: was G.1 Up until now, all of the relative clauses you’ve been working with involve a connection between an antecedent with a grammatical gender (or a plural form) and a relative pronoun that reflects that gender (or plural number): der Arbeitsplatz , den die Firma anbietet … die Erklärung , die sie uns gegeben hat … das Gerät , mit dem man kommunizieren kann … die Faktoren , die dabei wichtig sind … But consider sentences with antecedents and relative clauses such as these: There’s something that we need to talk about. I like everything that this band plays. Nothing that you say can stop me! There’s a lot that I don’t understand. (English speakers may not even think of these sentences as having a relative pronoun, since in each example it can be easily dropped: There’s something we need to talk about. / I like everything this band plays. / Nothing you say can stop me! / There’s a lot I don’t understand.) The problem: While something can connect with that in English, its German equivalent etwas doesn’t carry grammatical gender, nor does alles (everything) , nichts (nothing) or viel (a lot). And without grammatical gender and number, the usual connection between antecedent and relative pronoun cannot be established. Which is where was comes into play: When these and similar words function as antecedents — etwas nichts alles viel wenig — the relative pronoun is was : Gibt es irgend etwas , was wir für Sie tun können? Is there anything at all [that] we can do for you? Nichts , was er macht, scheint seinem Chef zu gefallen. Nothing [that] he does seems to please his boss. Auf diesem Gebiet gibt es wenig , was sie nicht schon gelesen hat. In this field, there is little [that] she hasn’t already read. G.1
G.2 Clausal antecedents + was Sometimes the antecedent of a relative clause is not a word but rather a statement or idea: He spends all his time at the office, which is hard on his family and friends. The relative clause in this sentence ( which is hard ... ) refers to the entire preceding statement He spends all his time …, rather than a single element within it. Since there is no specific antecedent that dictates gender or plural number, German uses was to connect the clauses: Er verbringt seine ganze Zeit im Büro , was für seine Familie und seine Freunde sehr schwer ist. Additional examples: Die Chefin hat die Arbeit meiner Kollegin kritisiert, was diese sehr irritiert hat. The boss criticized the work of my colleague, which really irritated her. Lukas findet seine Freizeit wichtiger als seine Arbeit, was ein älterer Kollege von ihm nicht versteht. Lukas considers his leisure time more important than his work, which an older colleague of his doesn't understand. G.2
G.3 Wo-compounds in relative clauses Like all other relative pronouns, was can be used in combination with prepositions. Consider how this works in English: Is there something [that] you’re scared of? There’s a lot [that] I have to think about right now. It’s everything [that] we were hoping for!* German deals with this the same way it deals with questions involving prepositions and was , namely, by using a wo- compound: Gibt es etwas , wovor du Angst hast? Im Moment gibt es viel , woran ich denken muss. Es ist alles , worauf wir gehofft haben! In other words: If the antecedent requires you to use was , and the relative clause calls for a preposition to be joined to the relative pronoun, a wo- compound is substituted for the was + preposition combination. This also applies to sentences in which the entire first clause serves as the antecedent (as discussed in G.2 ): Im kommenden Sommer arbeite ich in Berlin , worauf ich mich echt freue. * Notice in all three examples that the prepositions appear off by themselves, regardless of whether that is included or deleted; and that if you try to connect the preposition with the relative pronoun at the beginning of the relative clause, it will only work if you replace that with which . Once again: Good luck trying to explain this to a non-native speaker. G.3

## H. Relative pronouns: Summary
H. Relative pronouns: Summary German uses relative clauses in much the same way that English does, namely, to provide information about a word or idea by means of a clause that immediately follows it. How that clause is formed depends on just a few basic points: The connecting element in the second clause — the relative pronoun — reflects the gender (or plural number) of the word being modified; and its case is determined by its grammatical function in the relative clause. Another way of looking at this: The antecedent tells you which vertical column to choose from in the table below; and the grammatical function of the relative pronoun indicates which horizontal row to use. masculine feminine neuter plural Nominative der die das die Accusative den die das die Dative dem der dem denen Genitive dessen deren dessen deren Since a relative clause is a dependent clause, the conjugated verb ( V 1 ) appears at the end: ... das Geld, das ich verdient habe . If the relative pronoun is the object of a preposition (e.g., the chair on which you’re sitting ), the preposition comes first in the relative clause, followed by the relative pronoun object in the appropriate case, and then the rest of the information: ... der Stuhl, auf dem du sitzt. If the antecedent is not a gendered or plural noun, but rather a word such as viel, wenig, etwas, nichts, or alles — or the entire preceding phrase — then the relative pronoun was is used. If was functions as the object of a preposition in the relative clause, a wo- compound replaces was and the preposition. H
