--- Thema 10.1: Was ist Kindheit? ---

## A. Review: Verbs in independent clauses
A. Review: Verbs in independent clauses Verbs can appear in three formats in an independent clause: in the Present or Simple Past tense, with an ending that corresponds to the grammatical subject ( V 1 ) as an infinitive ( V 2 ), paired with a modal verb as a past participle ( V 2 ), paired with haben or sein to form the Perfect tense A.1 English word order normally places a conjugated verb ( V 1 ) after the subject of the clause, no matter how the sentence begins. When she was a child, she wanted to play video games all day. Despite the weather, we went running with some friends. Although it was a difficult decision, he has never regretted it. But as you learned in Chapter 8, German word order is different: Regardless of what the first element in the clause is, the second element is normally the conjugated verb ( V 1 ). If the subject of the clause is not the first element, then it comes after V 1 , not before it. Notice how this plays out in the translations of the sentences above: Als sie ein Kind war, wollte sie den ganzen Tag Videospiele spielen. Trotz des Wetters sind wir mit einigen Freunden joggen gegangen. Obwohl es eine schwere Entscheidung war, hat er sie nie bereut. A.1
A.2 The position of the second category of verbs ( V 2 ) in German also differs from English. Whereas English calls for V 2 to follow more or less directly after V 1 , German places the V 2 element at the very end of an independent clause. Compare: Als sie ein Kind war, wollte sie den ganzen Tag Videospiele spielen . When she was a child, she wanted to play video games all day. Trotz des Wetters sind wir mit einigen Freunden joggen gegangen . Despite the weather we have gone running with some friends. Obwohl es eine schwere Entscheidung war, hat er sie nie bereut . Although it was a difficult decision, he has never regretted it. A.2

## B. Verbs in dependent clauses
B. Verbs in dependent clauses B.1 Independent clauses vs. dependent clauses The word order rules reviewed above apply to independent clauses, i.e., clauses that can stand alone by themselves as a complete thought, regardless of length. Sie hatte keine Zeit. She didn’t have time. Ich glaube das. I believe that. Kennst du das Kind? Do you know the child? Dependent clauses, on the other hand, are attached to independent clauses, and appear in one of three formats: an infinitive clause: Sie hatte keine Zeit, mit uns ins Museum zu gehen. She didn’t have time to go with us to the museum. a subordinate clause: Ich glaube, dass sie Biologie studiert. I believe that she’s studying biology. a relative clause: Kennst du das Kind, das da im Sandkasten spielt? Do you know the child who is playing there in the sandbox? Infinitive clauses (a) were introduced in Chapter 8; relative clauses, shown in (c), will be explained in Chapter 13. This chapter deals with the second example of dependent clauses, shown in (b): subordinate clauses. B.1
B.2 Subordinate clauses / Subordinating conjunctions Subordinate clauses are information units that are joined to independent clauses by way of subordinating conjunctions, such as dass (that), weil (because) and obwohl (although). The conjunctions express the relationship of the dependent clause to the independent clause: She chose her major because she's interested in the subject . Now she realizes that it will also help her get a really good job. . The chief difference between English and German in subordinate clauses is the position of the conjugated verb ( V 1 ). Unlike English clauses, where V 1 remains in its usual position following the subject, the conjugated verb in a German subordinate clause moves to the very end of the clause. This happens regardless of whether V 1 is the main verb, a modal verb, or the auxiliary in the Perfect tense. It’s as if the subordinating conjunction “pushes” the conjugated verb to the very end of the clause. Notice how this works in the following sentences, in which the subject and V 1 in the dependent clauses are highlighted: Michelle und Tom haben mir gesagt, dass sie heute Abend zu Hause bleiben . Michelle and Tom told me that they are staying at home tonight. Michelle bleibt da, weil sie leider ganz krank geworden ist . Michelle is staying there because she has unfortunately become quite sick . Er bleibt zu Hause und lernt, obwohl er ausgehen will . He's staying at home and studying even though he wants to go out. A few additional points: Only V 1 moves to a different position; the rest of the elements — including V 2 — stay in their usual position. If V 1 is a separable-prefix verb, the prefix remains attached to the main verb. If a sentence begins with a subordinate clause, that clause is considered the “first element,” and the conjugated verb ( V 1 ) of the rest of the sentence follows immediately after it. These word order rules apply to both statements and questions. The “verb-at-the-end” rule is easy enough to articulate, but it requires a good deal of work — noticing it in texts and when people speak, trying it out, etc. — before it comes naturally, at least for speakers whose native language calls for a different word order. B.2
B.3 The subordinating conjunctions you’ll need to know — many of which are included in the core vocabulary for this Thema — are listed here in their order of frequency in German: dass that als when [in reference to the past] wenn if, when(ever) weil because während * while damit * so that, in order that obwohl although, even though nachdem after bevor before indem by ...ing Most of these show a one-to-one correspondence with their English equivalents: Bevor is used just like before (Before you go, …); dass means that, as in I hope that you can join us; and obwohl corresponds directly to although. But several German conjunctions require special attention: Wenn, als and ob (if, when, whenever and whether) don’t line up quite as neatly with their English equivalents, and will be looked at separately in Thema 2 of this chapter (Struktur C). Indem (by ...ing) deviates even more from English — there is, in fact, no one-word translation for it — and will be explained in Thema 4 (Struktur I). * You've encountered während as a genitive preposition meaning during , but it also can serve as a subordinating conjunction with the related meaning while . And you're familiar with damit as a da- compound, meaning with it / with them, but the subjordinating conjunction damit is an altogether different word, meaning so that, in order that . B.3

## C. Subordinate clauses: wenn
C. Subordinate clauses: wenn It would be nice if there were a one-to-one correspondence between English when and German wenn , but alas, there isn't. English when is used in a variety of contexts for which German requires different words; and as you learned in Thema 1 of this chapter, wenn can mean not only when(ever) but also if. To sort this out, we will look first at the distinctions for when that require different words in German; and then at wenn when it means if . C.1 Wenn | als As you saw earlier in this chapter, wenn means when (or whenever ) in reference to the present or future, just as in English Wenn du da bist, geht alles besser. When(ever) you’re here, everything goes better. Wenn du da bist, können wir beginnen. When you’re here, we can begin. But when is also used in English when referring to the past: When I was a child, … When my family moved, … When the music began, … When we visited Vienna last year, … For all of these — whether referring to one point in the past (when the music began) or to an extended period of time in the past (when I was a child) — German uses als : Als ich ein Kind war, … Als meine Familie umgezogen ist, … Als die Musik anfing, … Als wir letztes Jahr Wien besuchten, … For English speakers who use when to refer to either the present or the past, learning to make this distinction usually requires considerable time and practice — but it's very important to do so when conveying information to German speakers, who rely on als and wenn to alert them to temporal differences: Als es wärmer wurde, gingen wir joggen. When it got warmer, we went jogging. Wenn es wärmer wird, gehen wir joggen. When(ever) it gets warmer, we go jogging. / When it gets warmer, we’ll go jogging. C.1
C.2 wenn | wann As the first word in each of the questions in Übung 10.11 should remind you, German distinguishes between when in statements ( wenn ) and when in questions ( wann ). The good news here is that there’s no further distinction necessary between past and present: The question form of when is always Wann? Wann sollen Kinder ins Bett gehen? Wann hat er angefangen, sich für Sprachen zu interessieren? Wann hast du dein erstes Handy gekriegt? Wann beginnt dein Sommerurlaub? C.2
C.3 wenn | ob German also uses wenn in contexts where English uses a conditional if , as in: If that happens … . The problem here is that if in English can also be used in place of whether to refer to multiple options, as in I don't know whether/if it's going to rain or not, and for that meaning, German requires ob . Here are some more examples in English to help clarify these distinctions: If (conditional) is part of a sequence that expresses a condition and a consequence: If X, then Y. If you don’t get more sleep, you’re going to get sick. If you had told me that earlier, everything would have worked out. If the economy of Germany continues to outpace that of its neighbors, … This is different in meaning from if (whether): I don’t know if I can go with you (or not). Did you hear if we’re having a quiz tomorrow (or not)? She hasn’t told me if she wants to join the team (or not). In this second set of sentences, if can be replaced with whether: I don’t know whether I can go with you (or not). Did you hear whether we’re having a quiz tomorrow (or not)? She hasn’t told me whether she wants to join team (or not). But not in the first set: * Whether you don’t get more sleep, … * Whether you had told me that earlier, … Once you understand these two uses of if , supplying the correct German word is straightforward: if (conditional) → wenn if (whether) → ob C.3

## D. Subordinate clauses: Indirect questions
D. Subordinate clauses: Indirect questions D.1 Indirect »wer-wie-was« questions By now you're familiar with question words that introduce »wer-wie-was« questions: wann warum was was für wer/wen/wem wie wie viel wie viele wo woher wohin Wie heißt du? Wann hat die Party begonnen? Was gibt es heute zum Mittagessen? Mit wem hat er gesprochen? Was für ein Zimmer hast du? Wo kriegt man spätabends was zu essen? These in turn can be embedded in statements (or even other questions) as subordinate clauses, with the question word functioning as the subordinating conjunction. And as in all subordinate clauses, V 1 appears at the end: Ich habe leider vergessen, wie du heißt . Ich habe keine Ahnung° , wann die Party begonnen hat . Weißt du, was es heute zum Mittagessen gibt ? Er hat mir nicht gesagt, mit wem er gesprochen hat . Du weißt genau, was für ein Zimmer ich habe . Kannst du mir sagen, wo man spätabends was zu essen kriegt ? One further point: When a question beginning with Wann …? is embedded in another sentence, it retains wann as the subordinating conjunction. Weißt du, wann das Konzert anfängt? Ich habe vergessen, wann wir die nächste Prüfung haben. The following clauses often introduce indirect questions beginning with wann : Ich habe vergessen, … Ich habe keine Ahnung, … / Ich weiß (nicht), … / Ich weiß genau, … Kannst du mir sagen, …? / Ich kann dir (nicht) sagen, … D.1
D.2 Indirect “Verb-first” questions Questions that begin with a verb have the same word order as “wer-wie-was” questions, but without the introductory question word: Heißt er Marcel oder Markus? Ist die Party in seinem Zimmer? Wohnt er auf dem Campus oder in der Stadt? Hat er uns eingeladen? Kommt Jessica auch? These questions can also be embedded in other sentences (both statements and questions) by using the subordinating conjunction ob . Here as well, V 1 moves to the end of the resulting subordinate clause: Ich möchte wissen, ob er Marcel oder Markus heißt . Weißt du, ob die Party in seinem Zimmer ist ? Ich habe vergessen, ob er auf dem Campus oder in der Stadt wohnt . Kannst du mir sagen, ob er uns eingeladen hat ? Ich habe keine Ahnung° , ob Jessica mitkommt . Notice that these sentences make use of the same “frames” as indirect “wer-wie-was” questions: Ich weiß (nicht), … / Kannst du mir sagen, …? Ich habe vergessen, … D.2

## E. Review: Adjective endings
E. Review: Adjective endings Before working with the adjective endings for the genitive case in Thema 4, it may help to review the adjective endings for the other three cases. (We know you’ve been through this already in chapters 3-8, but for most people learning German, adjective endings pose a challenge. If you feel you don’t need this, you can skip to Übung 10.18 and continue from there.) Keep in mind is that German has two sets of endings for adjectives: Strong endings, which provide information regarding gender and case much like definite articles: Masculine Feminine Neuter Plural Nom. -er -e -es -e Acc. -en -e -es -e Dat. -em -er -em -en Weak endings, which are much simpler: Masculine Feminine Neuter Plural Nom. -e -e -e -en Acc. -en -e -e -en Dat. -en -en -en -en Knowing which ones to use depends on the kind of noun phrase you’re dealing with: der -word + adjective + noun ein -word + adjective + noun [no article] adjective + noun The following sections will review each of these in turn. E.1 Der-word + adjective + noun der/die/das dies- jed- ( singular ) welch- all- ( plural ) The endings on der- words provide precise grammatical information, so that only the weak endings are needed on adjectives following them. Applying this principle to some core vocabulary words from this Thema , with der/die/das standing in for the other der- words: Masculine Feminine Neuter Plural Nom. der neu e Lehrer die frei e Wahl das alt e Paar die schwer en Fälle Acc. den neu en Lehrer die frei e Wahl das alt e Paar die schwer en Fälle Dat. dem neu en Lehrer der frei en Wahl dem alt en Paar den schwer en Fälle n E.1
E.2 Ein-word + adjective + noun ein/eine/ein kein mein dein Ihr sein ihr ( her ) unser euer ihr ( their ) When ein- words have no ending — as in the masculine nominative singular, neuter nominative singular and neuter accusative singular — any adjectives following them must take strong endings. In all other contexts — including their plural forms — ein -words have endings analogous to der -words, and consequently any adjectives following them take weak endings. Notice in the chart below how this plays out across cases, genders, singular and plural forms: Masculine Feminine Neuter Plural Nom. ein neu er Gast ein e kurz e Frage ein groß es Auto kein e hoh en Berge Acc. ein en neu en Gast ein e kurz e Frage ein groß es Auto kein e hoh en Berge Dat. ein em neu en Gast ein er kurz en Frage ein em groß en Auto kein en hoh en Bergen A few examples from Schöne Tage by Axel Hacke: Nur einmal im Leben haben sie einen ersten Schultag … Sie haben eine wunderschöne Feier gemacht. »Ach, was hatten wir heute für einen schönen Tag , Felix!« E.2
E.3 [No article] Adjective + noun When an adjective appears with no preceding article — which happens frequently with plural nouns (new friends, interesting ideas) and with non-specific phrases such as guter Geschmack (good taste) — the adjective must provide the grammatical information that the articles would normally supply, using the strong endings for all genders and cases: Masculine Feminine Neuter Plural Nom. stark er Wind neu e Technik schnell es Essen gut e Gründe Acc. stark en Wind neu e Technik schnell es Essen gut e Gründe Dat. stark em Wind neu er Technik schnell em Essen gut en Gründe n E.3

## F. Adjectival nouns
F. Adjectival nouns In phrases such as The rich and famous, English uses adjectives in place of nouns, sometimes referred to as adjectival nouns . Phrases such as The important thing or What’s important here are similar; even if they contain a noun (such as thing ), the focus is on the adjective. The following discussion explains how these two kinds of adjectival nouns are formed and used in German. F.1 Thanks to the gender/number distinctions in German adjective endings, adjectival nouns can refer specifically to a man, a woman, or a group of people. In this usage, the adjective is capitalized (indicating its function as a noun), and the endings follow exactly the same patterns you’ve reviewed in the previous sections, just as if they were followed by a noun (e.g., Mann / Frau / Leute ). Using the adjective jugendlich ( adolescent, young ) from this section’s vocabulary: ein Jugendlich er a (male) adolescent/young person eine Jugendlich e a (female) adolescent/young person dieser Jugendlich e this (male) adolescent/young person jede Jugendlich e every (female) adolescent/young person die Jugendlich en the adolescents, the young people manche Jugendlich en some adolescents, some young people Jugendlich e adolescents, young people für eine Jugendlich e / für einen Jugendlich en / für Jugendlich e for a (female) adolescent / for a (male) adolescent / for adolescents mit einer Jugendlich en / mit einem Jugendlich en / mit Jugendlich en with a (female) adolescent / with a (male) adolescent / with adolescents Theoretically, you could deploy just about any adjective this way: der/die Kranke (the sick man/woman) , die Glücklichen (the happy people) , and so forth, in place of saying der kranke Mann / die kranke Frau or die glücklichen Leute. In practice, however, certain adjectival nouns have become the the preferred means for expressing certain meanings — and these are the ones you should concentrate on: bekannt well-known der Bekannt e eine Bekannt e Bekannt e the (male) acquaintance a (female) acquaintance acquaintances deutsch German die Deutsch en ein Deutsch er ein e Deutsch e Deutsch e the Germans a German (man) a German (woman) Germans fremd strange, foreign der Fremd e eine Fremd e Fremd e the (male) stranger a (female) stranger strangers The following words also appear frequently in this construction: arm poor die Arm en mit den Arm en the poor (people) with the poor (people) erwachsen grown (up) der Erwachsen e ein Erwachsen er eine Erwachsen e Erwachsen e the (male) adult a (male) adult a (female) adult adults reich rich die Reich e der Reich e außer den Reich en the rich woman the rich man except for the rich (people) verwandt related meine Verwandt en ein Verwandt er eine Verwandt e my relatives a (male) relative a (female) relative F.1
F.2 English uses thing, that or what along with an adjective to refer to concepts or abstractions: the important thing … what’s important here … that which is important … To express these phrases, German uses adjectival nouns with the neuter definite article das (nominative and accusative) or dem (dative): Nom. das Wichtig e the important thing / what’s important Acc. das Wichtig e Dat. dem Wichtig en If you want to elaborate on phrases like this (as in the important thing about … ), use the preposition an , followed by the dative case: Das Gut e an ihnen … The good thing about them … Das Interessant e an diesem Kurs … What’s interesting about this course … Warum siehst du nur das Negativ e an meinen Ideen? Why do you only see the negative side of my ideas? Of the adjectives you’ve learned so far, the following often appear in this construction: das Besondere das Echte das Historische das Interessante das Negative / das Positive das Persönliche das Schöne das Schwierige das Tolle das Wesentliche das Wichtige F.2

## G. Genitive adjective endings: der- / ein-words
G. Genitive adjective endings: der- / ein-words This one is easy: All adjectives between an article and a noun in the genitive case take the ending -en. A few examples from the topics you’ve been dealing with in this chapter: das Alter des jüngst en Kindes the age of the youngest child die Produzentin einer bekannt en Kindersendung the producer of a well known children’s TV program während des erst en Schultages during the first day of school eines dieser jung en Kinder one of these young children trotz der eigen en Familiengeschichte in spite of one’s own family history Notice that the -en ending applies to adjectives following der- words as well as ein- words, just like adjectives in the dative case. This is consistent with what you've learned regarding the interaction of articles and adjective endings: As long as the article supplies the required grammatical information (case, gender, number), the adjective takes a weak ending, either -e or -en. Since all articles in the genitive case do that, only the weak ending -en is needed. This completes the chart of weak endings for all four cases: Masculine Feminine Neuter Plural Nom. -e -e -e -en Acc. -en -e -e -en Dat. -en -en -en -en Gen. -en -en -en -en G

## H. Unpreceded adjectives + Genitive
H. Unpreceded adjectives + Genitive H.1 Genitive: Strong adjective endings In a genitive phrase where no article precedes the noun, such as the number of unemployed people , English uses the word of to indicate the connection between number and unemployed people . German, on the other hand, relies on the grammatical precision of its adjective endings to show this connection, in this case the strong genitive plural ending -er: Die Zahl arbeitslos er Menschen ... The strong genitive endings for masculine, feminine, neuter and plural nouns are as follows (including a reminder that masculine and neuter nouns add an -(e)s to the noun in the genitive case): Masculine Feminine Neuter Plural Genitive -en … -(e)s -er -en … -(e)s -er H.1
H.2 Genitive: Plural vs. singular unpreceded adjectives Plural nouns in the genitive case are often preceded by an adjective with no article, which calls for the adjective ending -er. Die Perspektive ausländisch er Schulkinder ... The perspective of foreign-born school children ... die Meinung linksorientiert er Politiker the opinion of left-leaning politicians But this construction can be used with singular nouns as well, and is often found in the context of genitive prepositions, so that both the -en and -er endings appear: statt interessant er Unterhaltung instead of interesting entertainment wegen ständig en Drucks because of constant pressure die Grenzen menschlich er Hilfe the limits of human help die Entwicklung musikalisch en Talents the development of musical talent In case you’re looking for a full listing of the strong adjective endings, here they are, for all cases and genders: Masculine Feminine Neuter Plural Nom. -er -e -es -e Acc. -en -e -es -e Dat. -em -er -em -en Gen. -en … -(e)s -er -en … -(e)s -er H.2

## I. Indem
I. Indem Read the following statements in English and consider how you might express them in German: Niklas tried to fall asleep by counting sheep. My friends and I relax by playing board games. Anna hopes to beat the worst of the traffic by using her GPS. You see the problem: German verbs have no -ing form — so how do you express ideas that use the English structure by ___ing ? Enter indem. On its own, this subordinating conjunction doesn’t correspond to any single English word. But in combination with a subject in a subordinate clause (very often a pronoun referring to something in the independent clause) it expresses the same meaning as by ___ing : Niklas versuchte ..., indem er Schafe zählte. Meine Freunde und ich ..., indem wir Brettspiele spielen. Anna hofft ..., indem sie ihr Navi benutzt. A few points to keep in mind when using this structure: As in all German subordinate clauses, indem appears first, and the conjugated verb (V 1 ) moves to the end of the clause. The by ___ing structure in English shows no tense, but the German equivalent with indem has a conjugated verb that must do so, and normally reflects the tense of the verb in the independent clause. In English as well as in German, either the independent or the subordinate clause may come first in the sentence: Indem er Schafe zählte , versuchte Niklas ... By counting sheep, Niklas tried ... Indem sie ihr Navi benutzt , hofft Anna ... By using her GPS, Anna hopes ... I
