## A. Review: Ich war … / Ich hatte …
A. Review: Ich war … / Ich hatte … The Interaktionen above make use of verb forms that express what someone has done (or said or thought) in the past. You’ve already learned how to express the past tense with the two most commonly used verbs in German: sein ( to be ) and haben ( to have ). Here’s a quick review of those forms: sein singular plural first-person ich war wir waren second-person du warst [familiar] Sie waren [formal] ihr wart [familiar] Sie waren [formal] third-person er/der Mann war sie/die Frau war es/das Kind war sie/die Leute waren haben singular plural first-person ich hatte wir hatten second-person du hattest [familiar] Sie hatten [formal] ihr hattet [familiar] Sie hatten [formal] third-person er/der Mann hatte sie/die Frau hatte es/das Kind hatte sie/die Leute hatten A

## B. Talking about the past: Simple Past vs. Perfect
B. Talking about the past: Simple Past vs. Perfect B.1 The past tense verbs you’ve just reviewed are analogous in form and usage to had and was in English, examples of what is often called the Simple Past because it requires only one word. But you may have noticed in the exercises above that war was sometimes translated as has been , using a compound form rather than a single word. English has both options, as these examples show: one-word option compound options I drove home. (a) I was driving home. (b) I have driven home. We took a trip. (a) We were taking a trip. (b) We have taken a trip. He bought a car. (a) He was buying a car. (b) He has bought a car. German also has compound options for the past tense, consisting of an auxiliary and a past participle, analogous in form to have driven and has bought . This tense is usually referred to as the Perfect tense (German: Perfekt ), since it denotes an action or state that is “perfected” in the sense of being finished, i.e., not continuing into the present. Compare these two sentences: Simple Past: Er kaufte ein Auto. He bought a car. Perfect: Er hat ein Auto gekauft . He bought a car. There are two important things to notice here: Meaning: In German, the Simple Past and the Perfect mean virtually the same thing. They both refer to actions or states that are “finished,” and are much closer in meaning to each other than the Simple Past (I did) and the Present Perfect (I have done) are in English. In other words, Er kaufte ein Auto and Er hat ein Auto gekauft can both be translated He bought a car. Word order: In the Perfect tense, the conjugated auxiliary verb ( hat ) appears at the beginning of the sentence with the subject, while the past participle comes at the very end. English occasionally allows some information to be inserted between the two, as in I have often done that , but German inserts all the accompanying information in the sentence between the auxiliary and the past participle: Ich habe das oft mit meiner Familie im Sommer gemacht . You’re probably thinking: If they’re so similar, why not just use the Simple Past? The answer has to do with slight differences in meaning (depending on context) and regional preferences for one or the other form. But for most verbs, in most contexts, the Perfect tense is the preferred way of referring to past events in everyday conversation. (The Simple Past forms war and hatte are notable exceptions, in that they are the preferred forms for most speakers.) This means that it's very important to learn the past participles used by the Perfekt , and to become familiar with the word order rules described above. B.1
B.2 Past participles Notice in these examples the relationship between the infinitive and the past participle of these verbs: eine Reise machen Wir haben eine kurze Reise gemacht . We took a short trip. ein Navi benutzen Ich habe zum ersten Mal ein Navi benutzt . I used a GPS for the first time. nach […] fahren Wir sind nach Heidelberg gefahren . We drove to Heidelberg. gefallen Die Stadt hat uns sehr gut gefallen . We really liked the city. dauern Die Fahrt nach Hause hat sechs Stunden gedauert . The drive back home took six hours. Most German past participles closely resemble their infinitive forms ( machen → gemacht / fahren → gefahren ) and are easily recognizable. B.2

## D. Ordinal numbers: first / second / third / …
D. Ordinal numbers: first / second / third / … Both German and English have similar systems for counting (one, two, three …) and for ordering and ranking (first, second, third …) . D.1 English ordering/ranking numerals make use of suffixes that echo the ending of the ordering/ranking word: ordering/ranking word ordering/ranking numeral first 1 st second 2 nd third 3 rd fourth 4 th fifth 5 th To write the ordering/ranking words in German, you simply add -t to the counting word (exceptions: ein/ erst-, drei/ dritt- and sieben/ siebt- ). To write the ordering/ranking numeral, place a period [ . ] immediately after the counting numeral: counting word counting numeral ordering/ranking word ordering/ranking numeral eins 1 erst- 1. zwei 2 zweit- 2. drei 3 dritt- 3. vier 4 viert- 4. fünf 5 fünft- 5. sechs 6 sechst- 6. sieben 7 siebt- 7. D.1
D.2 From 8 through 19, the pattern remains the same: Add -t to the counting word to create the ordering/ranking word. Starting with 20, however, you add -st to the counting word: counting word counting numeral ordering/ranking word ordering/ranking numeral zwanzig 20 zwanzigst- 20. einundzwanzig 21 einundzwanzigst- 21. zweiundzwanzig 22 zweiundzwanzigst- 22. Remember that from 20-29, the final element in the counting word in German is always -zwanzig (e.g., einundzwanzig, zweiundzwanzig, dreiundzwanzig, ...), which means that the final portion of all ordering/ranking words in the 20’s is zwanzigst- . ## E. Perfect tense: haben vs. sein
E. Perfect tense: haben vs. sein E.1 In the preceding Interaktionen you saw sentences in the Perfect tense that used haben as the auxiliary verb — Wann hast du deinen Führerschein gemacht ? When did you get your driver’s license? Die deutsche Autoindustrie hat 2010 fast sechs Millionen Autos hergestellt . The German auto industry produced almost six million cars in 2010. — but also sentences that used sein : Bist du nach der Prüfung gefahren ? Did you drive after the test? Wir sind nach Spanien geflogen . We flew to Spain. Both sets of sentences express past tense ideas, and can be rendered in English with either a single-word option or a compound-word option: Ich habe Führerschein gemacht! → I got my driver’s license! / I have gotten my driver’s license! Sie ist schon nach Hause gegangen. → She already went home. / She has already gone home. In case you’re interested: The use of sein in the Perfect tense mirrors English usage in Shakespeare’s day: Brutus: Hark! He is arrived . ( Julius Caesar , Act 4, Scene 2). E.1
E.2 So: When should you use haben and when should you use sein ? The choice depends almost entirely on the verb itself. Sein is used with the following verbs you’ve learned so far from the core vocabulary: an·kommen ist angekommen Wir sind später angekommen. auf·stehen ist aufgestanden Ich bin heute um 7 Uhr aufgestanden. aus·gehen ist ausgegangen Er ist am Samstag ausgegangen. bleiben ist geblieben Wir sind auf der Party geblieben. fahren ist gefahren Sie ist nach Hause gefahren. fliegen ist geflogen Wir sind nach Mallorca geflogen. gehen ist gegangen Warum bist du so früh gegangen? kommen ist gekommen Mir ist eine tolle Idee gekommen. laufen ist gelaufen Er ist zweimal um den Park gelaufen. reisen ist gereist Wann seid ihr nach Budapest gereist? werden ist geworden Sie ist Journalistin geworden. A few points about these verbs: Many of them (though not all) have to do with “moving from Point A to Point B.” Almost all verbs in German that convey this idea take sein as their auxiliary in the Perfect tense. Separable-prefix verbs derived from these verbs (e.g., zurück ·fliegen , mit ·kommen ) also use sein to form the Perfect tense, as long as they also express the idea of “moving from Point A to Point B.” Nach zwei Wochen in Italien sind wir zurückgeflogen . Warum bist du nicht mitgekommen ? Verbs that use sein as an auxiliary normally cannot take a direct object, i.e., they are intransitive. A few of the verbs listed above, however, can be used both with or without a direct object, and they use either haben or sein accordingly in the Perfect tense. The classic example is fahren : Ich bin mit Luca nach Bonn gefahren. I drove with Luca to Bonn. ( no direct object, Point A to Point B → sein ) Ich habe seinen Mercedes gefahren. I drove his Mercedes. (direct object → haben ) Double verbs with gehen — such as joggen gehen or shoppen gehen — are expressed in the perfect tense by using the infinitive of the first verb and the past participle of gehen : Wir sind gestern joggen gegangen . We went jogging yesterday. Bist du am Samstag shoppen gegangen ? Did you go shopping on Saturday? As you've no doubt noticed, past participles are listed for all verbs in the core vocabulary. Those that take sein in the Perfect tense include ist with the past participle. E.2

## F. Using the Dative Case
F. Using the Dative Case F.1 In a previous chapter you saw how case and grammatical gender work together in phrases that begin with accusative prepositions: gegen die Umwelt für ein Modell durch den Krieg ohne diese Technik um mein Auto durch jede Stadt Notice what happens to the articles when the accusative prepositions are replaced with the prepositions introduced in this chapter: mit der Umwelt with the environment außer einem Modell except for one model seit dem Krieg since the war bei dieser Technik in the case of this technology von meinem Auto from my car aus jeder Stadt out of every city These prepositions trigger the use of the dative case. Unlike the accusative case, the dative case affects all three genders, i.e., masculine, feminine and neuter dative articles all differ from their nominative (and accusative) forms. F.2 The dative case is used in other grammatical structures, which will be discussed later in this chapter and in Chapter 6. But its most frequent use is with the dative prepositions. Like prepositions in English, these can take on multiple meanings and are used idiomatically in many ways, but their basic meanings are as follows: ab starting ... ; from ... on ab diesem Moment aus out of aus dem Haus außer except for / besides außer der Frau bei in the case of [followed by a named establishment] at, near ____ [with a person] at the home of bei diesem Problem bei McDonald’s, bei Amazon bei meinem Freund gegenüber across from, facing in comparison with Die Garage ist gegenüber dem Haus. F.3 How do you say I’m going home ? German expresses this by using nach in a special idiom: Ich gehe nach Hause . The implied meaning for nach Hause is the home of the sentence subject: Sie geht nach Hause. She’s going home (i.e., to where she lives). Wir gehen nach Hause. We’re going home (i.e., to where we live). Easy enough. But what if it's someone else's home, as in I'm going to Kevin's house ? In this case you need to add an additional phrase using zu : Ich gehe zu Kevin nach Hause. Notice that the phrase nach Hause remains constant. You can add more information before and after it, but nothing can come between nach and Hause when they take the meaning (back) home. F.3
F.4 Three of the dative prepositions can combine with definite articles to form contractions: bei + dem = beim von + dem = vom zu + dem = zum zu + der = zur And speaking of zu : Zu appears in many idioms beyond its basic meaning of to . Here are some you already know or have seen: zu Hause at home zum Beispiel for example zum Frühstück for breakfast F.4
F.5 Plural nouns in the dative case All of the examples you’ve seen so far have featured singular nouns — but of course the dative case applies to plural nouns as well. Look carefully at the articles and nouns in these prepositional phrases: mit den Leuten with the people gegenüber diesen Autos across from these cars aus den Kriegen out of the wars seit zwei Jahrhunderten for two centuries zu meinen Kursen to my courses von welchen Regionen? from which regions? außer diesen Modellen except for these models As you can see, the articles and modifiers for dative plural are derived from den, that is, they all end with -en . Here are some more examples, using Kurs (plural: Kurse ): zu den Kursen zu dies en Kursen zu all en Kursen zu welch en Kursen zu kein en Kursen zu mein en Kursen zu kein en Kursen zu unser en Kursen If the plural of Kurs is Kurs e , why does it appear above as Kurs en ? The rule is simple: Plural nouns in the dative case add -n to their plural form, unless the plural form already ends with -n , or if it ends with an -s . Compare: die Leute → mit diesen Leute n die Männer → außer den Männer n BUT: die Autos → mit diesen Autos die Richtungen → aus diesen Richtungen It may help you to think of the plural dative not simply as den, but as den n F.5

## H. Dative Case: Verbs / Indirect Objects
H. Dative Case: Verbs / Indirect Objects H.1 Dative Verbs In Interaktion 5.33 you came across a sentence that reads Und ich helfe dir. Why dir instead of dich , since it's the object of helfen ? In German, a small number of verbs, including the core vocabulary verbs antworten , glauben and helfen , take dative rather than accusative objects. Warum antwortest du dem Mann nicht? Why don’t you answer the man? Glaubst du mir oder ihr ? Do you believe me or her? Wir helfen diesen Leuten. We’re helping these people. Other verbs, such as gefallen and gehören , also appear in most contexts with dative objects, similar to the way their English equivalents use to … Postmoderne Architektur gefällt diesen Leuten nicht. These people don’t like postmodernist architecture. (lit.: … doesn’t appeal to these people .) Das Geld gehört mir , nicht dir . The money belongs to me , not to you . You’ll find speakers and writers using gehören + zu , but this doesn't mean to belong to . For example, Er gehört zu mir does not mean He belongs to me (in the sense of ownership) but rather He belongs with me or He is with me . (An extrapolation of this is a sentence such as Wien gehört zu den schönsten Städten der Welt , which means Vienna is counted among the most beautiful cities in the world, or simply Vienna is one of ... .) All that to say: To express the literal meaning of X belongs to Y , you should use gehören with a dative object— X gehört Y —rather than gehören + zu . H.1
H.2 Dative: indirect objects H.2.1 To English speakers, the use of the dative case with a verb such as helfen makes sense if one thinks of helfen as giving help ( Hilfe geben ). In this phrase, help / Hilfe is a direct object (i.e., what you’re giving), and the person or entity receiving it is the indirect object (i.e., the recipient). In English, indirect objects are expressed in one of two ways: She’s giving food to the children . She’s giving the children food. German prefers the latter construction — using the position of the noun rather than to / zu — and places indirect object nouns and pronouns before direct object nouns: Sie gibt den Kindern Essen. Sie gibt ihnen Essen. H.2.1
H.2.2 Certain verbs frequently appear with indirect objects: Verbs like geben that denote giving or sharing . These verbs require three sentence elements: Someone who’s doing the giving (the subject: nominative); the object being given (accusative); and the recipient of the object (dative). Notice the relative position of the direct objects and indirect objects in these examples: Ich gebe dir 10 Euro. I’ll give you 10 euros. / I’ll give 10 Euros to you. Der Professor hat der Gruppe eine sehr schwere Aufgabe gegeben. The professor gave the group a very difficult assignment. Either the direct object or the indirect object can be the focus of a question, and that element serves as the question word: Was gibst du mir dafür? What will you give me for it? Wem gibst du das Geld? Who are you giving the money to? / To whom are you giving the money? Erklären usually involves these three elements as well, though it's possible to use it with just a direct object: Der Professor erklärt den Studenten die Aufgabe. The professor is explaining the assignment to the students. Sie hat mir die Situation erklärt. She explained the situation to me. Das erklärt alles! That explains everything! Sagen is used very frequently with the dative case, in the sense of saying something to someone , or telling someone something : Er sagt mir nie, was er denkt. He never tells me what he’s thinking. Hast du deinen Eltern alles gesagt? Have you told your parents everything? / Did you tell your parents everything? You can use sagen without specifying to whom something is being said, that is, with no indirect object in the dative case, in which case it means to say rather than to tell : Er sagt nie, was er denkt. He never says what he’s thinking. Hast du alles gesagt? Did you say everything? / Have you said everything? H.2.2

## I. Dative: Adjective Endings
I. Dative: Adjective Endings I.1 You may still be reeling from learning the adjective endings for the nominative and accusative cases, strong and weak, and you’re wondering what new horror awaits you regarding adjective endings for the dative case. Ready? Here it is: Adjectives following any article in the dative case take the ending -en . That’s it. Here are some samples from the Interaktionen you’ve read in this chapter, with the dative phrases underlined and the endings highlighted: Das war der Prototyp von der erst en Autobahn . Hast du die Prüfung beim erst en Mal bestanden? Hier sehen Sie eine Karte mit den wichtigst en Autobahnen . We could provide a chart to show this for all three genders and plural nouns, using all the der -words and ein- words, but every single adjective in it would have the same ending: -en. I.1
I.2 When there's no der- or ein- word in the noun phrase, things are admittedly not quite so simple, but here too the adjective endings are very consistent. As with the nominative and accusative cases, when a dative noun has no preceding article, the adjective ending itself approximates the dative article: masculine feminine neuter plural dem→ -em der→ -er dem→ -em den→ -en This is how these endings appear in actual phrases: masculine feminine neuter plural bei stark em Wind mit neu er Technik bei schwach em Licht von schnell en Autos Phrases with unpreceded adjectives often include plural nouns, and since the adjective ending for these ( -en ) is identical to the ending used after dative der- and ein- words, it’s easy to remember.
