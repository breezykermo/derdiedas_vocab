--- Thema 15.1: Klimakrise ---

## A. Subjunctive
A. Subjunctive A.1 Subjunctive mood Using the Subjunctive mood of a verb, you can … (1) speculate about something that is contrary to fact regarding the present or the past: If I were you … (but I’m not) If Goethe were alive today … (but he isn’t) If we had time today, we could … (but we don’t, so we can’t) If the professor had told us … (but she didn’t) I could have been a contender … (but I wasn’t) (2) convey hypothetical situations and wishes — which are not “true,” strictly speaking, but potentially possible: It would be wonderful if … We could have a surprise birthday party for her! (3) express polite requests and responses, pertaining to the present or the future: Would you mind … ? Could you please … ? Would you be so kind as to … ? A party? I would love to go! Notice how English expresses the subjunctive mood by deploying already-existing verb forms. Take were , for example: In some contexts, it expresses a past-tense state (we were there); but paired with if , it conveys a contrary-to-fact hypothesis about the present or future: If I were you, … . Similar examples could be made with had , could and would . In German, however, most verbs that appear in the Present subjunctive mood ( der Konjunktiv ) have a distinctive form, based on the Simple Past*: Simple past Subjunctive wurde → würde war → wäre hatte → hätte Würde was introduced in Chapter 9 ( H.1 ), and is by far the most frequently used subjunctive form you will encounter. The sections below review würde briefly, and then introduce the forms and uses of wäre and hätte , which after würde are the most frequently used subjunctive verbs in German. * Weak verbs (“t-verbs”) do not have a distinctive form for the Present subjunctive mood, and therefore usually appear as infinitives with würde when the subjunctive mood is required. More on this in Thema 2. A.1
A.2 würde A. Würde corresponds closely with would in English, when would takes on the meanings described above in A.1: speculation involving contrary-to-fact or hypothetical situations: I would help you if I had time. We would really like to be there, but … What would you do if …? It would be so great if you could … polite requests and responses: Would you mind showing me where …? I would be happy to. It’s right over there. B. You may already be familiar with other uses of would in English, but in case you’re not, this is a good place to mention them: Would can express habitual or repeated events in the past, similar to used to: My family would often take trips … In high school we would sometimes … Would can also refer to events that are in the “future” from the perspective of a point in the past: Harry would eventually find out that his real parents … Rachel Carson's »Silent Spring« would later lead to the creation of the EPA. German does not use würde to express these two uses of would — so be careful to distinguish between them and the hypothetical / speculative / polite meanings that würde properly conveys. C. Forms and use: würde would subjunctive stem: würd- ich würde (familiar) du (formal) Sie würdest würden er/sie/es würde wir würden (familiar) ihr (formal) Sie würdet würden sie würden Würden is the conjugated verb ( V 1 ) in a clause, usually in tandem with an infinitive ( V 2 ). In main clauses it is the second element: An deiner Stelle würde ich das nicht machen. In dependent clauses it is the final element: Es interessiert mich gar nicht, was du an meiner Stelle machen würdest . A.2

## B. Present time subjunctive
B. Present time subjunctive B.1 Present vs. Past subjunctive Technically, the subjunctive mood has no “tenses” — just two “times”: Present subjunctive (speculation, wishes and polite requests pertaining to the present and the future) and Past subjunctive (speculation and wishes pertaining to the past). Present subjunctive: Past subjunctive: If it weren’t raining today … If it hadn’t been raining yesterday … I wish I could help you, but … I wish I could have helped you, but … That would be difficult. That would have been difficult. Would you please … ? The Present subjunctive of strong and irregular verbs such as sein and haben is formed by adding umlaut and special endings to the Simple past stem, as the following sections explain in detail. B.1
B.2 wäre English expresses speculation and hypothesis by way of two different subjunctive constructions. Take to be, for example: That would be nice … If I were you … German expresses both of these with one form, based on war : Das wäre schön. That would be nice. Wenn ich an deiner Stelle wäre , … If I were you … [lit.: If I were in your position] Du wärest wohl glücklicher, wenn … You would probably be happier if … …, dann wären die Temperaturen nicht so hoch. … , then the temperatures wouldn't be so high. Here is the full set of forms: wäre would be subjunctive stem: wär- ich wäre (familiar) du (formal) Sie wärest wären er/sie/es wäre wir wären (familiar) ihr (formal) Sie wäret wären sie wären NB: In colloquial speech, wäre is often shortened to wär, and wärest to wärst . B.2
B.3 hätte English also uses two different subjunctive constructions, depending on the context, with the verb to have: That would have a major impact on greenhouse gases. If I had time, I would … Here too, German uses just one form, based on the Simple Past form of haben ( hatte ), to which it adds an umlaut and the same endings used with wäre: Wenn ich Zeit hätte , … If I had time, … Du hättest bessere Noten, wenn … You would have better grades if … …, dann hätten wir endlich eine Lösung! … , then we would finally have a solution! Here is the full set of forms: hätte would have subjunctive stem: hätt- ich hätte (familiar) du (formal) Sie hättest hätten er/sie/es hätte wir hätten (familiar) ihr (formal) Sie hättet hätten sie hätten Hätte is routinely shortened in colloquial speech: Ich hätt’s nicht gemacht . I wouldn’t have done it. B.3

--- Thema 15.2: Naturwissenschaft ---

## C. Wenn ..., dann ... / Subjunctive modal verbs
C. Wenn ..., dann ... / Subjunctive modal verbs C.1 Conditional sentences: Wenn ..., dann ... Conditional sentences express an if/then scenario. This can involve real conditions and consequences, in which case the verbs are expressed in the indicative mood: If it rains, (then) I’m not going running . But very often conditional sentences involve a contrary-to-fact or speculative hypothesis, and the verbs are expressed in the subjunctive mood: If it weren't raining, I would definitely go running with you. Really. A few more examples: Wenn ich zwischen Mathe und Informatik wählen müsste , (dann) würde ich mich wohl für Informatik entscheiden. If I had to choose between math and computer science, (then) I would probably go with computer science. Wenn die Wahl aber zwischen Mathe und Physik wäre, (dann) wüsste ich nicht so genau. But if the choice were between math and physics, (then) I wouldn't know exactly. There are just a few guidelines for if/then conditional sentences in German: The if -clause begins with wenn and is a dependent clause, with V 1 at the end; the then- clause serves as the main clause, with V 1 in second position. Either clause can appear first in the sentence (as in English). When the if- clause appears first, dann is often inserted between the two clauses, serving to separate the two V 1 elements. But this is optional: The back-to-back verbs aren’t as awkward in German as they might appear to an English speaker. If the consequence clause comes first, however, dann is not used. Compare: Wenn ich zwischen Mathe und Informatik wählen müsste , (dann) würde ich mich wohl für Informatik entscheiden. If I had to choose between math and computer science, (then) I would probably go with computer science. Ich würde mich wohl für Informatik entscheiden, wenn ich zwischen Informatik und Mathe wählen müsste . I would probably go with computer science, if I had to choose between computer science and math. C.1
C.2 möchte / könnte / müsste / dürfte // wüsste The Present Subjunctive mood is expressed in the modal verbs and wissen by adding an umlaut to their Simple Past forms: Infinitive Simple past Subjunctive mögen mochte → möchte können konnte → könnte müssen musste → müsste dürfen durfte → dürfte wissen wusste → wüsste Modal verbs function in the subjunctive mood exactly as they do in the indicative mood, with the conjugated modal verb ( V 1 ) complemented by a V 2 infinitive. Wissen takes either a direct object or a dependent clause. Wenn wir ein Oktoberfest hier hätten, könnte ich dir ein Dirndl kaufen ! If we had an Oktoberfest here, I could buy you a dirndl! Schön. Aber dann müsstest du natürlich eine Lederhose tragen . Nice. But then of course you’d have to wear Lederhosen. Gerne. Aber ich wüsste nicht, was ich dazu tragen sollte. Gladly. But I wouldn't know what to wear with them. You’re already familiar with möchte , but for the sake of review here is the full set of its forms: möchte would like to subjunctive stem: möcht- ich möchte (familiar) du (formal) Sie möchtest möchten er/sie/es möchte wir möchten (familiar) du (formal) Sie möchtet möchten sie möchten könnte Könnte means would be able to / could. English makes could do double duty: Present Subjunctive (We could do that later), as well as Simple Past (I could n’t go out last night). Könnte takes only the first of these two meanings; the second, as you’ve already learned, is expressed by konnte (with no umlaut). könnte would be able to / could subjunctive stem: könnt- ich könnte (familiar) du (formal) Sie könntest könnten er/sie/es könnte wir könnten (familiar) du (formal) Sie könntet könnten sie könnten müsste Müsste expresses speculation about what would have to be done in a hypothetical situation. Wenn wir morgen eine Prüfung in Bio hätten, müsste ich die ganze Nacht lernen. If we had a test tomorrow in biology, I would have to study all night. müsste would have (to) subjunctive stem: müsst- ich müsste (familiar) du (formal) Sie müsstest müssten er/sie/es müsste wir müssten (familiar) du (formal) Sie müsstet müssten sie müssten dürfte Dürfte expresses specualtion about what would be allowed / permissible in a hypothetical situation. Dürfte ich auf dem Campus überall parken, wenn ich ein Auto hätte? Would I be allowed to park everywhere on campus if I had a car? dürfte would be allowed (to) subjunctive stem: dürft- ich dürfte (familiar) du (formal) Sie dürftest dürften er/sie/es dürfte wir dürften (familiar) du (formal) Sie dürftet dürften sie dürften wüsste Wissen was introduced in Chapter 6 along with the modal verbs because it conjugates precisely the same way — and the same is true of its formation in the subjunctive mood: wüsste were to know / knew subjunctive stem: wüsst- ich wüsste (familiar) du (formal) Sie wüsstest wüssten er/sie/es wüsste wir wüssten (familiar) du (formal) Sie wüsstet wüssten sie wüssten Ich wünschte, wir wüssten, wann das Flugzeug landen soll. I wish we knew when the plane is supposed to land. Wenn ich die Antwort wüsste , dann würde ich nicht fragen, oder? If I knew the answer, I wouldn’t be asking, right? C.2
C.3 Modal verbs: sollte / wollte Sollen and wollen differ from the other modal verbs, in that their Present Subjunctive forms take no umlaut and are therefore identical with the Simple Past forms: Infinitive Simple past Subjunctive sollen sollte → sollte wollen wollte → wollte In contexts that express past time, sollte is in the indicative mood and means was supposed to. When the context indicates the present or future, sollte is in the subjunctive mood and means should . Ich sollte gestern meine Eltern anrufen, aber ich hatte keine Zeit. I was supposed to call my parents yesterday, but I didn’t have time. Also sollte ich mir heute wirklich etwas Zeit dafür nehmen. So I should really make some time for that today. Sollen in the indicative mood is often used to elicit practical advice, especially in questions: Was soll ich machen? What should I do? / ... am I supposed to do? The Present Subjunctive form sollte , on the other hand, is often used in general statements describing something that should be the case — but is perhaps unlikely to happen: Ich sollte echt früher aufstehen. I should really get up earlier. Du solltest nicht so viel rauchen. You shouldn't smoke so much. Wollte is used more frequently in the Simple Past than in the Present Subjunctive, but it sometimes appears in place of wünschte to introduce a hypothetical wish: Ich wollte , du könntest das verstehen. I wish you could understand that. C.3

## D. Subjunctive forms: Strong + Weak verbs
D. Subjunctive forms: Strong + Weak verbs D.1 Subjunctive II / würde + Infinitive The subjunctive forms you’ve worked with so far are often referred to as Subjunctive II forms, since they are derived from the Simple Past, the second principle part of a verb’s paradigm: I: Infinitve II: Simple past III: Past participle sein war gewesen haben hatte gehabt werden wurde geworden wissen wusste gewusst Like English subjunctives (if I were / that would be ), German subjunctives appear in Subjunctive II forms as well as in the combination würde + infinitive. German, however, uses the Subjunctive II forms almost exclusively with the small number of verbs you’ve worked with so far: sein / haben / werden / wissen and the modal verbs. With all other verbs — thousands of them — most German speakers prefer to use the würde + infinitive format. Since you will, however, occasionally come across Subjunctive II forms for other verbs — especially in literary texts — it will be helpful for you to know how to recognize them as such. To that end, the following two sections explain how strong and weak verbs are formed and used in the subjunctive mood. D.1
D.2 Subjunctive forms: Strong verbs The subjunctive form wäre (would be) illustrates the way that strong verbs in German express themselves in the subjunctive mood: Simple Past add umlaut (a / o / u) add endings sein → war w ä r- wär e The verb endings used with wäre appear with hätte and würde as well, and are very similar to other forms you already know: ich → -e du → -est / Sie → -en er/sie/es → -e wir → -en ihr → -et / Sie → -en sie → -en Applying these rules to other strong verbs yields the following forms: Simple past tense add umlaut add endings kommen → kam k ä m- käm e / käm est / … gehen → ging ging- ging e / ging est / … essen → aß ä ß- äß e / äß est / … schreiben → schrieb schrieb- schrieb e / schrieb est / … geben → gab g ä b- gäb e / gäb est / … Child’s play, right? If only. While this formula can be used with many strong verbs, there are numerous exceptions. Some strong verbs require a different vowel altogether (e.g., stehen → stand → stünde ), some allow more than one form (helfen → hälfe / hülfe ), some replace an expected umlauted ä with e (nennen → nannte → nennte ), and so on. More important, virtually all of these forms — with the exception of commonly used idioms such as es gäbe (there would be) — sound excessively formal, even archaic, in most contexts. For now, you should avoid using these forms, and rely instead on würde + infinitive for all verbs except werden, sein, haben, wissen and the modal verbs. D.2
D.3 Subjunctive forms: Weak verbs If strong verb subjunctive forms are sometimes too complicated to be useful, the subjunctive forms of weak verbs are too simple. Here too, the Subjunctive II forms consist of the Simple Past stem (spielen → spielt- ) and the endings found on all other Subjunctive II verbs. But as you can see, this means that these forms are, in fact, identical to the Simple Past: Infinitive Simple past Subjunctive II spielen spiel te / spiel test / … spiel te / spiel test / … sagen sag te / sag test / … sag te / sag test / … It’s conceivable that a time element in a sentence could make it clear that the verb is in the Present Subjunctive rather than in the Simple Past, but in practice this is rarely done. Instead, German speakers routinely use the würde + infinitive format with weak verbs to express the subjunctive mood: Ich wünschte, sie würde öfter Gitarre spielen . I wish she would play the guitar more often. Wenn er mir so was sagen würde , … If he said something like that to me, … D.3

## E. Past subjunctive
E. Past subjunctive The Past subjunctive expresses contrary-to-fact wishes and hypotheses (statements and questions) that refer to the past: Wenn wir das gewusst hätten , … If we had known that, … If we would have known that, … Ich wünschte, ich hätte mich anders verhalten . I wish I had acted differently. I wish I would have acted differently. Notice that German has just one version of the Past subjunctive (single-word auxiliary + past participle), while English speakers must choose between had or would have as the auxiliary, depending on the context. But remember: The single-word auxiliary in German can be either haben or sein , depending on the verb, and the following sections explain the use of each of these in turn. E.1 hätte … gemacht Any verb that uses haben to form the Perfect tense — habe … gearbeitet worked haben … enthalten contained hast … abgelehnt rejected — uses hätte to form the Past subjunctive: hätte … gearbeitet would have worked / had worked hätten … enthalten would have contained / had contained hättest … abgelehnt would have rejected / had rejected Notice the word order of these, depending on the type of clause in which they appear: Wenn du mehr daran gearbeitet hättest , … If you had worked on it more, … If you would have worked on it more, … Ich wünschte, ich hätte das Angebot nicht abgelehnt . I wish I wouldn’t have rejected the offer. I wish I hadn't rejected the offer. E.1
E.2 wäre … gegangen Verbs that take sein in the Perfect tense form the Past subjunctive with the auxiliary wäre . The rule is simple enough — yet difficult for many English speakers to use spontaneously, perhaps because wäre bears no resemblance at all to would have or had as used in English Past subjunctives: Ich wäre geblieben, wenn du da gewesen wärest. I would have stayed if you had been there. To learn this structure well, you’ll need to use it as often as possible to create meaningful sentences — perhaps by creating sentences first in English and thinking how to express them in German; or by creating short, simple sentences in German and picturing situations in which you’d use them in real conversations: Ich wäre früher aufgestanden, aber … I would have gotten up earlier, but … Das wäre nie passiert , wenn … That would never have happened if … Wir wären auch ausgegangen . We would have gone out too. Wenn du pünktlich angekommen wärest, … If you had arrived on time, … Das wäre super gewesen! That would have been awesome! E.2

## F. Past subjunctive: Modal verbs
F. Past subjunctive: Modal verbs Using modal verbs in the Past subjunctive allows you to express contrary-to-fact hypotheses, wishes and speculations with the added nuance of should have, could have and would have had to: You should have seen the game! I wish she could have gone with us. We would have had to wait 50 minutes for a table. F.1 Maybe I should have ... Sentences like these aren’t necessarily literary or high-register, but the syntax required to form them in German requires special attention. Similar to many Past subjunctive forms, it uses hätte as V 1 . And like all modal verb constructions, it requires a modal verb and an infinitive to convey the desired meaning: should have seen … → sollen / sehen could have gone … → können / gehen would have had to wait … → müssen / warten But there’s a twist: Instead of using the past participle of the modal verb, as one might expect, this construction calls for the infinitive of the modal verb, and places it after the other infinitive to form a “double infinitive” that functions as V 2 : hätte … sehen sollen hätte … gehen können hätte … warten müssen Putting all of this together: Wir hätten mehr für die Umwelt machen sollen. We should have done more for the environment. Ich wünschte, sie hätte mit uns gehen können. I wish she could have gone with us. Du hättest 45 Minuten auf einen Tisch warten müssen. You would have had to wait 45 minutes for a table. Notice that V 1 is hätte in all three examples, even though the second example uses gehen , which takes sein as its auxiliary. This is because the choice of the auxiliary depends on the modal verb — not on the modal infinitive — and all modal verbs take haben in the Perfect tense and hätte in the Past subjunctive. [The modal verbs sollen, müssen and können are shown in these examples because they are used much more frequently in the Past subjunctive than dürfen , mögen , and wollen .] F.1
F.2 woulda, coulda, shoulda This is a reminder of the important (but easily forgotten) difference between certain Past subjunctive forms in English and German. In English, there is an obvious structural parallel with these three verbs: She would have gone with us. She could have gone with us. She should have gone with us. But German draws an important syntactic distinction between would have (the standard Past Subjunctive form) and could have / should have (Past Subjunctive modal verbs): would have is rendered in German with hätte/wäre + past participle. Sie wäre mit uns gegangen. She would have gone with us. could have / should have are rendered with hätte + double infinitive Sie hätte mit uns gehen können. She could have gone with us. Sie hätte mit uns gehen sollen. She should have gone with us. Here's an additional example, using a verb that takes haben as its auxiliary: Ich hätte dich angesprochen. I would have spoken to you. Ich hätte dich ansprechen sollen. I should have spoken to you. Ich hätte dich ansprechen können. I could have spoken to you. F.2

## G. as if
G. as if G.1 als ob The two-part conjunction als ob means as if and introduces a dependent clause. The verb in an als ob clause is frequently in the subjunctive mood, either Present or Past, and, as in all dependent clauses, V 1 appears at the end. Tobias sieht aus, als ob er krank wäre. Tobias looks as if he were sick. Annika fühlte sich, als ob sie gerade einen Marathon gelaufen wäre. Annika felt as if she had just run a marathon. Felix spricht manchmal so, als ob er alles wüsste. Sometimes Felix talks as if he knew everything. Nadine tut häufig so, als ob ihr alles egal wäre. Nadine often acts as if nothing mattered to her. Als ob clauses are preceded by a relatively small number of standard openers. Sometimes so is inserted into these, meaning in such a way as to … , but this is optional. Er/Sie/Es sieht (so) aus, als ob … He/She/It looks as if … Sie/Er fühlt sich (so), als ob … She/He feels as if … Sie sprechen (so), als ob … They talk as if … Es scheint (so), als ob … It seems as if … One such opener makes use of the verb tun along with so to convey the meaning to act: Sie tut so, als ob … She’s acting as if … G.1
G.2 als wäre … / als hätte … In written German, as if clauses can be constructed with the conjugated verb (V 1 ) taking the place of ob , while retaining the meaning of als ob. Compare: Es sieht so aus, als ob die Bäume miteinander kommunizieren würden . Es sieht so aus, als würden die Bäume miteinander kommunizieren. A few more examples: Der Philosoph tut so, als ob seine Theorie die einzige wäre , die … Der Philosoph tut so, als wäre seine Theorie die einzige, die … Es sah aus, als ob die Firma trotz ihrer Geldprobleme eine Zukunft hätte . ## H. Indirect Speech
H. Indirect Speech H.1 Indirect speech: Subjunctive II So far, you’ve been using the subjunctive mood to convey contrary-to-fact hypotheses, wishes and polite requests, much as English does: contrary-to-fact hypotheses Wenn ich du wäre , … Wenn ich Zeit hätte , … Wenn ich mehr schlafen könnte , … If I were you, … If I had time, … If I could sleep more, … wishes Sie wünschte, das Semester wäre schon zu Ende. Er wünschte, sie würde ihm eine SMS schicken. She wishes the semester were already finished. He wishes she would send him a text message. polite requests Könnten Sie mir sagen, wo …? Würden Sie bitte langsamer sprechen? Could you tell me where …? Would you please speak more slowly? But German makes use of the subjunctive mood for another purpose as well: Indirect speech (indirekte Rede). If you're not familiar with indirect speech, it may help to compare it with direct speech, which is simply what someone says, i.e., the words contained within quotation marks: Julian said, “I don't have any time today.” Indirect speech, on the other hand, occurs when someone removes the quotation marks, so to speak, and relays that information by weaving it into a sentence of their own: Julian said that he didn't have time today. Notice the differences: The subject becomes third-person rather than first-person (I → he) , and the verb can also change (don't → didn't) . In German, the pronoun changes the same way, but the verb can be cast in the subjunctive, which conveys the idea that these are not my words — this is what X said: Julian sagte, er hätte heute keine Zeit. In this context, hätte does not mean would have , in a conditional or hypothetical sense, but simply has . The subjunctive signals that This is what Julian said, as distinct from what the person quoting Julian may or may not think. If the speaker/writer doesn’t care to make this distinction, then the information quoted can be expressed with the indicative mood: Julian sagte, er hat heute keine Zeit. On a surface level, the meaning is the same. But the choice of the indicative mood over the subjunctive provides an additional nuance, in that the speaker/writer is communicating that she has no need to emphasize her objectivity or distance in conveying this information. H.1
H.2 Indirect speech: Tenses / Questions A. When English speakers use indirect speech, they're forced to navigate between indicative and subjunctive, present and past, when framing the indirect quote, since there's no one right way to frame it. You can say, for example, Julian said he doesn’t have time or Julian said he didn’t have time — and refer in both versions to today , i.e., present time. German is more precise here: The tense of the original information is mirrored in the time of the subjunctive: direct indirect Past (Simple Past, Perfect, Past Perfect) »Ich war da.« »Ich bin da gewesen.« »Ich war da gewesen.« Lisa sagte, sie wäre da gewesen . (Past subjunctive) Present »Ich bin glücklich.« Lisa sagte, sie wäre glücklich. (Present subjunctive) Future »Ich werde da sein.« Lisa sagte, sie würde da sein . (werde + infinitive → würde + infinitive) Granted, this precision is most evident in distinguishing between past and present tense verbs, and less so between present and future tenses. With verbs other than sein, haben, wissen and the modal verbs, German normally uses the würde + infinitive format for Present subjunctive — which is also used to express the Future tense in indirect speech. But as you’ve learned, the Future tense is seldom used, so that this ambiguity rarely poses a problem. (And keep in mind: If there’s no need for the speaker to distance themselves from the message they’re quoting, the indirect speech can be framed in the indicative mood. In that case, the tense of the direct version is simply carried over into the indirect version.) B. Conveying questions in indirect speech depends on the kind of question posed directly: »Wer-wie-was« questions (e.g., Wo ist Jonas? ) use the question word as a conjunction in a dependent clause: Maxine fragte, wo Jonas wäre. / …, wo Jonas ist. Verb-first questions (e.g., Ist Jonas schon da? ) use ob as a conjunction in a dependent clause: Maxine fragte, ob Jonas schon da wäre. / …, ob Jonas schon da ist. H.2
H.3 Subjunctive I Using the subjunctive mood to relate indirect speech creates a sense of distance between the original source of information and the person relating it — an objectivity that connotes I’m not saying this is true or that I agree or disagree, it’s just what the person said. Nowhere is this nuance more necessary than in journalistic reporting, in which a writer must be careful to distinguish between someone else's speech and the writer's own commentary and opinions regarding it. German has a verb form that conveys this objectivity very clearly, and it is used almost exclusively in journalistic contexts for this purpose. It’s called Subjunctive I , since it’s derived from the first principle part of the verb, i.e., the infinitive: Infinitive Subjunctive I werden → er/sie werde sein → er/sie sei haben → er/sie habe müssen → er/sie müsse (It’s the simplest verb form to conjugate: Infinitive minus the (e)n, with an -e ending for third-person singular — except for sein, which simply uses the stem. No added umlauts, no deleted umlauts, no stem changes, in fact no changes at all.) While a full paradigm exists for all the other persons and numbers, the third-person singular forms are what you’ll see and use 99% of the time, for two reasons: Subjunctive I is used almost exclusively to convey indirect speech in journalistic reporting, citing third-person (singular and plural) sources: The chancellor is of the opinion that … / Scientists have recently expressed concern, stating that … The third-person plural forms of Subjunctive I are identical to the indicative mood, which means that the “objective” nuance provided by the subjunctive mood is lost; as a result, writers use Subjunctive II forms for this purpose instead. The exercises below provide examples and comparisons of direct and indirect quotes, showing you how journalistic writing makes use of Subjunctive I, and letting you put it to use yourself. H.3
