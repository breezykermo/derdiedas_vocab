- EXPERT: a fluent, near-native German speaker. Patient, warm, precise.
- LEARNER: a student at exactly this point in the course (end of Kapitel 7). Keen and
  chatty, but makes the kinds of mistakes real learners make at this stage.

How the corrections work (the core of the episode):
- The LEARNER makes roughly 12-20 mistakes over the episode. Most should target the
  Kapitel 7 grammar points below; the rest should come from earlier chapters (wrong
  article/gender, verb endings, plural forms, word order, a vocabulary mix-up).
  Mistakes must be plausible for this level, never random nonsense.
- Every mistake gets corrected — none slip past. The EXPERT either recasts it ("Ah, du
  meinst: ...") or stops briefly to explain the rule in one or two sentences, then gives
  a correct model sentence.
- The LEARNER then repeats the corrected sentence and, where it fits, makes a new
  sentence using the same rule correctly, so the listener hears the right form at least
  twice for every time they hear the wrong one.
- Make the mistake and its correction unmistakable to a listener: the EXPERT should say
  clearly what was wrong (e.g. "Nicht 'der Stadt' — 'die Stadt'. Stadt ist feminin.").
- Now and then the EXPERT asks the listener directly: "Hast du den Fehler gehört?
  Mach eine Pause und korrigiere ihn!" — then gives the answer.
- Corrections are kind and encouraging; the LEARNER sometimes self-corrects or
  asks "Warum?" to prompt an explanation.

Language — IMMERSION: the episode is in German. This is for listening comprehension,
so the listener should hear as much German as possible.
- Speak only German. No English at all.
- Make meaning clear IN GERMAN: slow down, repeat, rephrase with known words, give
  examples and contrasts, and have the LEARNER check understanding in German
  ("Was bedeutet ...?", "Also ... heißt ...?").
- Explain grammar in simple German by SHOWING: contrast the wrong and the right form,
  then give several more correct examples. Grammar terms that are close to English are
  fine: Nominativ, Akkusativ, Dativ, Genitiv, maskulin, feminin, neutral, Singular,
  Plural, Artikel, Verb, Endung, Präsens, Perfekt, Präteritum, Konjunktiv, Passiv.
- Never translate whole sentences into English and never switch to English for a whole
  explanation.
- The grammar explanations and glosses further down this source are in English for
  YOUR reference only. Don't read or quote them; teach those points in German.

Hard constraints:

  chapter's list plus the earlier-chapter list), along with names, numbers, obvious

- German grammar: correct sentences use ONLY structures from Kapitel 1-7 (see the

--- Thema 7.1: Freizeitsport ---

## A. Prepositions: Accusative / Dative / Variable
A. Prepositions: Accusative / Dative / Variable A.1 Before introducing a third category of prepositions, let's review what you've learned so far: ab aus außer bei durch für gegen mit nach ohne seit um von zu Every one of these triggers the use of a particular case: Some require the accusative, some require the dative. When you talk about running through the forest, using durch , for example, the forest must be in the accusative case: durch den Wald . But when you say that everyone showed up except my friend, außer triggers the dative case: außer meinem Freund . A.1
A.2 The prepositions in the preceding exercises have historical and/or etymological ties with the accusative or dative case, but there’s no meaning-based reason why mit requires the dative case, or why ohne takes the accusative case. There is a third group of prepositions, however, for which meaning and case are very closely linked, and these are known as variable prepositions. What sets variable prepositions apart from the others? Their default meaning has to do with location . They trigger either the dative case or the accusative case, depending on whether this location refers to where something is , or if it's the destination toward which something is moving . A.2

## B. Variable prepositions: Dative
B. Variable prepositions: Dative B.1 When variable prepositions are used to indicate where something or someone is located, or where something is taking place, they trigger the dative case. A few examples: Wir spielen manchmal auf dem Tennisplatz . In meinem Dorf gibt es ein Fußballteam und ein Handballteam. Sie geht oft in dem Wald joggen. Er steht zwischen dem Netz und seiner Partnerin . Eine Liste von allen Teams hängt an der Wand . Notice that each of the prepositional phrases above answers the question Wo? Wo spielen wir? Auf dem Tennisplatz. Wo gibt es zwei Teams? In meinem Dorf. Wo geht sie joggen? In dem Wald / im Wald. Wo steht er? Zwischen dem Netz und seiner Partnerin. Wo hängt die Liste? An der Wand. B.1
B.2 Several verbs you’ve learned from the core vocabulary are frequently used with these prepositions to indicate location: hängen to hang / to be hanging liegen to lie / to be lying / to be located sein to be sitzen to sit / to be sitting stehen to stand / to be standing But just about any verb can be used with variable prepositions in the dative case, as long as the meaning has to do with where something is, or is taking place: Sie arbeitet seit Jahren in dieser Stadt . Man findet Tennisplätze überall auf dem Campus . Gibt es eine Sporthalle in der Nähe vom Wohnheim? Er hat das Auto vor der Garage geparkt . Essen wir in der Küche oder im Esszimmer ? NB: When in and an are followed by dem , they normally form the contractions im and am . B.2
B.3 When an , in and vor are used in time expressions, they take the dative case: an (referring to days of the week) : Mein Yoga-Kurs beginnt [an + dem] → am Montag. Sie hat am 21. Oktober Geburtstag. in referring to months and seasons : Sie hat [in + dem] → im Oktober Geburtstag. Das heißt, sie ist im Herbst° geboren. referring to future points in time : Die Prüfung ist in drei Wochen. Wir reisen in einem Monat nach Salzburg. vor (placed before the noun: ago ) Wir haben das vor drei Wochen gelernt. (three weeks ago) Ich habe den Film vor einigen Jahren gesehen. (a few years ago) N.B.: Where English uses in with numerals to specify a year (e.g., in 1989), German states the year without a preposition or, in more formal settings, prefaces it with the phrase im Jahre : Deutschland hat 2014 die Weltmeisterschaft gewonnen, aber 2018 und 2022 ... na ja° . Die Wiedervereinigung° hat im Jahr 1989 angefangen. B.3

## C. Variable prepositions: Accusative
C. Variable prepositions: Accusative C.1 When variable prepositions are used with verbs denoting motion toward a destination — much the same way that English uses into as opposed to in — they trigger the accusative case: Wir fahren heute in die Stadt . Hängen wir das an die Wand oder an die Tür ? Er hat seinen Computer auf den Tisch gestellt. Wann musst du in deine Vorlesung gehen? This is obvious with verbs of motion such as gehen and fahren, when the accompanying prepositional phrase indicates the destination of that motion. But it also occurs with verbs that denote putting and placing, all of which require a direct object as well as a destination, i.e., where you’re putting or placing the direct object: stellen to place legen to lay hängen to hang tun [colloquial/informal] to put Sentences using verbs and prepositions like this require two accusative phrases or pronouns — one for the direct object, and another for the object in the prepositional phrase that identifies the destination. Ich tue meine Sportschuhe immer vor die Tür . Warum hängst du diese komischen° Bilder an die Wand ? Du kannst deine Bücher auf mein Bett legen. NB: When in , an and auf are followed by das, they normally form the contractions ins , ans and aufs : Morgen gehen wir ins Konzert. Er hat seine Bücher aufs Bett gelegt. C.1
C.2 These prepositional phrases answer the question Wohin ? (to/toward where) Wohin tust du deine Schuhe? —Vor die Tür. Wohin hängst du die Bilder? —An die Wand. Wohin kann ich die Bücher legen? —Auf das Bett. / Aufs Bett. You've now learned three different German words for where: from where (origin) woher? Woher kommst du? where (location) wo? Wo wohnst du? to where (destination) wohin ? Wohin gehst du? Wohin hängst du das Bild? Keeping these distinctions in mind may initially be a challenge for you — unless you're the sort of person who routinely uses words like whither and whence — so it's a good idea to create sentences like these on your own while focusing on the distinctions, in order to develop a feel for matching your intended meaning with the correct word for where . C.2

## D. Reflexive pronouns
D. Reflexive pronouns Sometimes the object of a verb or preposition is the same entity as the sentence subject — in other words, the object reflects the subject: After the game, the players weren’t too happy with themselves . I gave myself two months to train for the race. She needs to buy new running shoes for herself . Where do you see yourself in five years? English uses a special set of reflexive pronouns for this, each corresponding to a particular subject: They → themselves , I → myself , she → herself , we → ourselves , and so on. German is simpler: It uses the same object pronouns you’ve already learned for ich , wir , du and ihr. The only exceptions are third-person reflexive objects and reflexive objects with Sie (singular and plural), and for all of these German uses the special reflexive pronoun sich . This section explains in detail how reflexive pronouns function when they serve as accusative objects, and the following sections (E.1, E.2), explain their use as dative objects. D.1 Accusative reflexive pronouns You already know the grammatical contexts that require accusative objects: direct objects objects of accusative prepositions (durch / für / gegen / ohne / um) Whenever these accusative objects represent the same entity as the subject, the following reflexive pronouns are used: singular plural nominative accusative nominative accusative ich mich wir uns du Sie dich sich ihr Sie euch sich er sie es sich sich sich sie sich A few examples: Ich frage mich manchmal, warum ... Wo siehst du dich in 10 Jahren? Sie hat sich vorgestellt. D.1
D.2 Accusative reflexive pronouns are closely associated with a special category of verbs known as reflexive verbs, so named because their meaning depends on the combination of the verb with the reflexive pronoun. Three of these verbs were introduced in the core vocabulary of this section: (sich) fühlen to feel (sich) entscheiden t o decide (sich) verletzen to injure (oneself) Since the English meaning for these verbs may or may not contain a reflexive pronoun, German reflexive verbs require special attention as you learn them: Wie fühlst du dich heute? Wie fühlen Sie sich heute? Wie fühlt ihr euch heute? How are you feeling today? Wir fühlen uns ganz wohl. We’re feeling pretty good. Hast du dich entschieden? Haben Sie sich entschieden? Habt ihr euch entschieden? Have you decided? Ich kann mich einfach nicht entscheiden. I simply can’t decide. Wie hast du dich verletzt? Wie haben Sie sich verletzt? Wie habt ihr euch verletzt? How did you injure yourself? Man kann sich beim Fußball verletzen. You can injure yourself playing soccer. Noch ein Beispiel — aus der Corona-Zeit (2020): husten° sich verschlucken° D.2
D.3 Word order with reflexive pronouns All you need to know at this point regarding word order for reflexive pronouns is that sich and other reflexive pronouns come right after the conjugated verb — that is, before any nouns, prepositional phrases or other modifiers. There’s one important exception: If the sentence subject is a pronoun that appears after the verb (as it does in questions: Hat er ...?), then that subject pronoun comes first (Hat er sich ...?). A few examples: Er fühlt sich heute nicht wohl. Sie entscheidet sich jetzt. Fragst du dich manchmal, warum …? This rule applies in the Perfect tense and with modal verbs as well: Er hat sich gestern auch nicht wohl gefühlt. Sie muss sich jetzt entscheiden . Hast du dich je gefragt , warum …? Notice, by the way, that in the Perfect tense examples here, the auxiliary verb is haben . In fact, the auxiliary is always haben in sentences with accusative reflexive verbs, since there’s always an accusative object in play — and sein can only serve as the auxiliary when there’s no accusative object. (French speakers, beware: This is precisely the opposite of the auxiliary rule for the passé composé .) D.3
E.1 Dative reflexive pronouns You've learned that dative objects are used ... with dative prepositions (ab / aus / außer / bei / …) with dative verbs (helfen / antworten / gefallen / …) to indicate an indirect object, e.g., giving something to someone . Here are a few examples: Ich kaufe meiner Freundin etwas zu essen. Er kann dem Kind nicht helfen. Sie organisiert ein Fest bei Freunden . Dative reflexive pronouns can be substituted in all of these sentences, indicating that the dative object is identical to the sentence subject: Ich kaufe mir etwas zu essen. I’m buying myself something to eat. Er kann sich nicht helfen. He can’t help himself. Sie organisiert ein Fest bei sich . She’s organizing a celebration at her place. Here too, the dative reflexive pronouns are identical to the dative pronouns, with the exception of sich, which is used with all third-person subjects (er, sie, es, sie [pl.]) and Sie. Here is the full list: singular plural nominative dative nominative dative ich mir wir uns du Sie dir sich ihr Sie euch sich er sie es sich sich sich sie NB: The only reflexive pronouns that distinguish between accusative and dative are mich/mir and dich/dir ; all the others are the same for both accusative and dative: uns / euch / sich . E.1
E.2 Dative reflexive pronouns are often used in situations where the sentence subject is doing (e.g., buying, cooking, making ) something for himself or herself: Die Frau kauft sich ein Buch. The woman is buying herself a book. Wir kaufen uns einen neuen Fernseher. We’re buying ourselves a new TV. Ich habe mir einen Salat gemacht. I made myself a salad. Du kannst dir etwas zum Mittagessen kochen, oder? You can cook yourself something for lunch, can’t you? It may help to think of this as a phrase with three elements: sich etwas kaufen sich etwas kochen sich etwas machen Etwas refers here to the direct object of the verb, i.e., what is being done ( bought , cooked , made ); while sich indicates that the recipient of that object is the sentence subject, and is therefore expressed with a dative reflexive pronoun. E.2
E.3 A small number of German verbs require a dative reflexive pronoun and an accusative direct object to express a specific meaning. [NB: Their English equivalents usually do not include a reflexive pronoun.] sich etwas leisten to afford [something] sich etwas vor•stellen to imagine [something] (You’ve already learned vor•stellen with the meaning to introduce; but in the phrase sich etwas vor•stellen , it takes on the meaning to imagine/picture [something] to oneself .) Here are some examples using these two verb phrases, with the reflexive pronoun highlighted in bold and the accusative object underlined: Wie kannst du dir ein Auto leisten? How can you afford a car? Ich kann mir einen Abend in NYC leisten, aber nicht ein ganzes Wochenende. I can afford an evening in NYC, but not a whole weekend. Können wir uns dieses Haus leisten? Can we afford this house? [NB: sich etwas leisten almost always appears with können .] Wie hast du dir das Marathon-Training vorgestellt? How did you picture training for the marathon? / What did you imagine the marathon training would be like? Ich stelle mir meinen Traumjob so vor: … I picture my dream job like this: … Kannst du dir das vorstellen? Can you imagine that? E.3

## F. Verb + preposition combinations
F. Verb + preposition combinations The prepositions you’ve learned have been introduced with their basic meanings, e.g. mit ( with ), für ( for ), auf ( on ). But prepositions can take on significantly different meanings when used in combination with other elements. Consider two of the core vocabulary items in this section: handeln + von to deal with, to be about warten + auf to wait for You’ve learned that the basic meaning of von is of or by . But used with handeln, it translates into English as with or about . Similarly, auf by itself normally means on (top of) . But in conjunction with warten, it takes on the English meaning for, as in to wait for . German (like English) has hundreds of such combinations, many of which make use of elements you’ve already learned: arbeiten + an [D] to work on bestehen + aus to consist of denken + an [A] to think about, have in mind halten + von to think of (i.e., have an opinion of) schreiben + über [A] to write about (The designations [D] and [A] indicate that the variable preposition in the combination takes the dative or accusative case, respectively — more on that in the F.2. ) A few examples: Der Film »An Affair to Remember« handelt von einer Liebesbeziehung. Ein Mann wartet auf eine Frau ganz oben auf dem Empire-State-Building, und denkt die ganze Zeit an sie. Hast du den Film gesehen? Was hältst du davon ? Der Film »Sleepless in Seattle« besteht aus dieser alten Geschichte und einer neuen Romanze dazu. F.1
F.2 Two points to keep in mind regarding verb + preposition combinations: Meaning: Combinations like these are “fixed,” in the sense that a specific meaning requires a specific combination, as distinct from the individual meanings of the constituent elements. In other words, even though you know that halten means to hold/stop , and that von means of/by , you also need to know that the combination halten + von means to think of in the sense of to have an opinion of: Was hältst du von meiner Idee? Case: Since variable prepositions in verb + preposition combinations do not refer to location or motion, the rules concerning the choice of dative and accusative don’t apply. Instead, the combination itself stipulates the case to be used with the preposition whenever it appears in that particular combination. To take an example: When auf is used with verbs such as legen or liegen , you have to consider the motion/location issue to decide whether the accusative or the dative case should be used. But when auf appears with warten , meaning to wait for, it always takes the accusative case [A] . Similarly, an can take either the accusative or the dative case when used with hängen, depending on whether someone is hanging something somewhere (motion) or something is already hanging somewhere (location). But when an appears with arbeiten to mean to work on , it always takes the dative case [D] . Accusative prepositions ( durch , für , …) and dative prepositions ( aus , außer , …), on the other hand, always take their respective cases, regardless of context. In other words, von in handeln + von takes the dative case simply because von is a dative preposition. F.2

## G. Reflexive verb + preposition combinations
G. Reflexive verb + preposition combinations G.1 A number of fixed combinations involve reflexive verbs, including the following items in this chapter’s core vocabulary: sich beschäftigen + mit to occupy oneself with, deal with sich erinnern + an [A] to remember sich freuen + über [A] to be happy about sich freuen + auf [A] to look forward to sich interessieren + für to be interested in The guidelines in the discussion above ( F.2 ) apply to these verbs as well: Their meanings are dependent on a specific combination of elements. In order to convey that meaning, all three elements — verb + reflexive pronoun + preposition — must be present. When variable prepositions are used in these combinations, they take a fixed case, as indicated in the listings above. One additional guideline: Unless otherwise noted, the reflexive pronoun in these phrases is in the accusative case. Read through the following examples of the verbs listed above, and notice how the highlighted elements are used: Wir beschäftigen uns heute mit einem neuen Thema. Today we’re dealing with (occupying ourselves with / devoting ourselves to) a new topic. Erinnerst du dich an seinen Namen? Do you remember his name? Ich freue mich sehr über die gute Nachricht! I’m really happy about the good news! Sie freut sich auf das Wochenende. She’s looking forward to the weekend. Interessieren Sie sich für Jazz? Are you interested in jazz? G.1
G.2 Sometimes the English translation lines up nicely with the analogous elements in German, so that there's no need to think of the combination as a special case: Ich beschäftige mich mit … I concern myself with … But many of these combinations are structurally very different from their English equivalents: Ich erinnere mich an ihn. I remember him. You can see the problem here: It’s tempting for English speakers to equate remember with erinnern and use the latter with a simple direct object, as English usage dictates. Remember and erinnern are related, of course; but to say I remember you , German requires a verb, a reflexive pronoun and a preposition followed by the accusative case: Ich erinnere mich an dich . G.2

## H. Da-compounds
H. Da-compounds You've already learned several words that are formed by joining da- to a preposition: dafür for it dagegen against it damit with it This section explains these formations in more detail, and includes all of the da- compounds introduced in this and previous chapters. H.1 The English definitions given above suggest how da- compounds work. The use of the pronoun it in all of them indicates that something has been mentioned (or will be mentioned), and that the da- compound says something about it by way of the preposition. In German, da- takes the place of it . But it could actually be plural as well as singular, and can refer to something concrete or abstract. This means that the definitions above should actually include many more elements: dafür for it, for this, for that, for them, for these, for those (i.e., things, ideas, situations, etc. ) What da- cannot refer to, however, is people. In other words, while dafür can take all of the meanings above, it cannot mean for her , for him , or for them, i.e., for those people. To express these meanings, German requires the personal pronouns, showing the appropriate case and number: für sie (sing.), für ihn , für sie (pl.). Accordingly, when a German speaker hears or sees a pronoun after a preposition, especially sie and ihn , the assumption is that the pronoun refers to a person. If the prepositional object is not a person, however, you should use a da- compound to make that clear. And while this may seem like additional work, it’s actually a handy shortcut. Think about it: When you use pronouns, you have to sort out the case required by the preposition (accusative? dative? variable? motion or location?), not to mention the gender and number of the noun to which it refers. With da- compounds, by contrast, there are no choices to make — it's just da- . Prepositions that begin with a vowel have an -r- inserted between the two elements: da r an / da r in / da r um / da r aus / da r über. H.1
H.2 The examples above make use of the literal meanings you’ve learned for these prepositions. But remember that prepositions can have a wide range of meaning. In some contexts they take their literal meanings ( auf = on , für = for , etc.), but they often take meanings that differ substantially from these when they appear in fixed combinations with certain verbs, as discussed in a previous section of this chapter: sich interessieren + für to be interested + in warten + auf to wait + for denken + an to think + about This is why the core vocabulary glossary includes an ellipsis as part of the translation of da- compounds: darauf on that/it, … The dots are a reminder that a da- compound may potentially have many other meanings besides its literal definition. Here are some examples, using the verb/preposition combinations above: Kants Philosophie ist schwer, aber ich interessiere mich sehr dafür . Kant’s philosophy is difficult, but I’m really interested in it. Er hat schon vor drei Tagen gesagt, er wollte mir die Information schicken. Ich warte noch immer darauf. He said three days ago he wanted to send me the information. I’m still waiting for it. Montag habe ich zwei Prüfungen, aber ich will jetzt nicht daran denken . Monday I have two tests, but I don’t want to think about them/that now. H.2

## I. Wo-compounds
I. Wo-compounds I.1 How do you ask questions using verb/preposition combinations? English has various means of doing this, ranging from casual to formal. The casual version places the preposition at the end of the sentence, while the formal version places the preposition at the very beginning of the question: Who are you thinking about ? Whom are you thinking about ? About whom are you thinking? In German, there’s only one option: Prepositional phrases linked with verbs must appear as a unit at the beginning of the question, regardless of the level of formality ( du or Sie ): Auf wen warten Sie? Auf wen wartest du? For whom are you waiting? Who(m) are you waiting for? Mit wem haben Sie gesprochen? Mit wem hast du gesprochen? With whom were you talking? Who(m) were you talking with? An wen denken Sie gerade? An wen denkst du gerade? About whom are you thinking? Who(m) are you thinking about? And where informal English allows you to use who as a prepositional object, while more formal registers call for whom, German always uses wen (accusative) or wem (dative), as required by the grammatical context: Neben wem hast du im Bus gesessen? (location → Dative) Who(m) were you sitting next to on the bus? Neben wen hast du dich hingesetzt? (motion → Accusative) Who(m) did you sit down next to? An wen erinnern Sie sich? ( sich erinnern + an [Accusative]) Who(m) do you remember? I.1
I.2 What about prepositional phrases that involve things rather than people? What are you waiting for ? For what are you waiting? To express these, German uses a construction called a wo- compound — and as you might guess, wo- compounds are very similar to da- compounds. The wo- stands for the generic what that would appear in an English question: Worauf warten Sie noch? What are you still waiting for ? Womit hast du das geschrieben? What did you write that with ? Woran denken Sie gerade? What are you thinking about right now? Like da- compounds, wo- compounds involving prepositions that begin with a vowel insert an -r- between the two elements: wo r an / wo r auf / wo r über. ( Wo- compounds appear much less frequently in German than da- compounds, but they are included here because of their similarity to da- compounds — and because they’re useful in putting verb + preposition combinations into practice.) I.2
I.3 Special case: halten + von Since you know that halten + von has to do with expressing opinions, you may find yourself trying to formulate the question What do you think of X? with a wo- compound. It doesn't work. In German as in English, the question focuses on What? rather than on the prepositional phrase [of X], so that the proper formulation begins with Was , and the prepositional phrase beginning with von expresses the topic under consideration [X]: Was hältst du von [X]? Was halten Sie von [X]? What do you think of [X]? If [X] (e.g., your dorm room, Hegelian metaphysics, the weather) has already been mentioned in the conversation, it can be referred to with a da- compound: Was hältst du davon ? Was halten Sie davon ? What do you think of it / that / them? (But remember: If [X] is a person or people, you must use a personal pronoun rather than a da- compound.) The response to Was halten Sie von [X]? can be formulated with halten + für , or with verbs such as finden / glauben / denken : Ich halte [X] für ... Ich finde [X] ... Ich denke, [X] ... I think [X] is ... / I consider [X] to be ... I find [X] ... I think [X] is ... I.3
