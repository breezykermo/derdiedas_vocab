--- Thema 9.1: Reif für die Insel ---

## A. Infinitive clauses
A. Infinitive clauses A.1. A clause is a group of words that contains a verb. In Thema 2 of Chapter 8 a distinction was drawn between independent clauses and dependent clauses: independent clauses can stand alone, while a dependent clause must be connected with an independent clause in order to form a complete sentence. German has three types of dependent clauses: infinitive clauses, which make use of an infinitive rather than a conjugated verb, since there is no subject in the clause; subordinate clauses, which begin with subordinating conjunctions such as dass (that), weil (because) and wenn (if), and include a subject and a conjugated verb; and relative clauses, which contain a relative pronoun connecting the clause to an element in the independent clause, as well as a subject (unless the pronoun itself is the subject) and a conjugated verb. This chapter reviews infinitive clauses (from Chapter 8) and introduces a special (but very commonly used) kind of infinitive clause. The other dependent clauses — subordinate and relative — will be discussed in chapters 10 and 14. A.1
A.2 Review: Infinitive clauses Infinitive clauses have an especially close relationship with independent clauses, since the subject of the independent clause normally serves as the subject for the infinitive clause as well. Hast du Zeit, mit mir zu sprechen? Do you have time to talk with me? [ “You” is clearly the subject of have time, but is likewise the assumed subject of to talk with me. ] Remember that in German infinitive clauses, the infinitive appears at the end of the clause: Ich habe immer Zeit, mit dir zu sprechen . I always have time to talk with you . It’s possible to have an infinitive clause that consists solely of the infinitive — Ich habe immer Zeit zu sprechen! I always have time to talk! — but very often there are additional elements that are associated with the infinitive and precede it within the clause. If there are no additional elements, no comma is needed to separate the clauses; but if additional elements are present, a comma is often used to show where the independent clause ends and the infinitive clause begins. Es macht mir Spaß zu schwimmen . (no additional elements → no comma) But: Es macht mir immer Spaß, im Meer zu schwimmen. Es macht mir besonders viel Spaß, morgens im Meer zu schwimmen. The examples above show how easily some German infinitive clauses map onto English: Ich habe nicht genug Zeit zu schlafen. I don't have enough time to sleep . This is not always the case, however. Infinitive clauses following auf•hören (to stop, cease) translate as _____ing: Ich kann nicht aufhören, an dich zu denken. I can't stop thinking about you. A.2
B.1 In order to: um … zu … To express a goal, English uses the phrase in order along with an infinitive clause: I went to the library in order to get a book. He flew to Florida in order to visit his grandparents. She stayed up all night in order to work on this problem. German conveys this by inserting um at the beginning of an infinitive clause: Ich bin in die Bibliothek gegangen, um ein Buch zu holen. Er ist nach Florida geflogen, um seine Großeltern zu besuchen. Sie ist die ganze Nacht aufgeblieben, um an diesem Problem zu arbeiten. In this usage, um is not a preposition, but rather a part of the construction um … zu … (in order to). NB: With um … zu … constructions, a comma is always inserted before um . B.1

--- Thema 9.2: Urlaub auf Mallorca ---

## (C.1)
C.1 Referring to the Past In Chapter 5 you learned how to express ideas relating to the past by using the Perfect tense, which is formed by combining an auxiliary verb ( haben or sein ) with a past participle: Letztes Jahr bin ich nach Paris gereist. Last year I traveled to Paris. Wir haben den Eiffelturm gesehen und Versailles besucht . We saw the Eiffel Tower and visited Versailles. But prior to that you learned that frequently used verbs such as sein, haben and the modal verbs make use of a different past-tense form, consisting of only one word — war / hatte / wollte / musste — which is therefore known as the Simple Past. In the following sections you'll learn in more detail how this tense is formed and used. C.1
C.2 Simple Past vs. Perfect If both the Perfect and the Simple Past refer to the past, how do you decide which one to use? Here are some basic guidelines: The Simple Past and the Perfect are much more similar in German than in English, and in many contexts are interchangeable. The Perfect tense is most often used for making brief conversational references to the past — e.g., "What did you do over the weekend?" / "I finally finished my problem set." / "We saw a movie." — with almost all verbs. Almost, but not all: Many German speakers prefer to use the Simple Past with sein , haben , the modal verbs ( wollen / müssen / dürfen / sollen / können ) and several commonly used verbs such as wissen and sagen. Regional differences show up here: Speakers in southern Germany, Switzerland and Austria often prefer to use the Perfect tense with these verbs as well. When the Simple Past is used consistently in a narrative context — a novel, a memoir, an anecdote — it creates a sense of moving through narrative time, telling a story, drawing the reader into that story. Sometimes it's referred to as the "narrative past," in contrast to the "conversational past." In short: When making references to the past in conversation, you should use the Perfect tense (auxiliary verb + past participle), except with the commonly used verbs cited above. But to convey a narrative (particularly in writing), the Simple Past is the preferred tense for all verbs. C.2

## D. Simple past I: modal verbs / mixed verbs
D. Simple past I: modal verbs / mixed verbs Now for the forms: Different verbs have different ways of forming the Simple Past in German. Some add one set of endings to the verb stem, others add a different set, some show changes to the verb stem itself, while others don't. E. ## F. Simple Past III: Strong verbs
F. Simple Past III: Strong verbs Now the real fun begins. While the vast majority of verbs in German are weak verbs with predictable Simple Past forms, the most commonly used verbs in German — and English, for that matter — happen to be strong verbs. Strong verbs in German change their stems (think of English: do → did; write → wrote; eat → ate ), which means that learning the forms requires frequent use and review. F.1 Review: sein The most commonly used strong verb is, of course, sein — for which you’ve already learned the Simple Past tense forms: singular plural first-person ich war wir waren second-person du warst (familiar) Sie waren (formal) ihr wart (familiar) Sie waren (formal) third-person er/der Mann war sie/die Frau war es/das Kind war sie/die Leute waren Several important features of strong verbs in the Simple Past can be seen here: The stem undergoes a change from the infinitive form: sein → war. The conjugations for ich and er / sie / es are identical: just the modified stem, with no ending. The conjugations for du / Sie / wir / ihr / sie (pl.) are formed by adding their respective endings to the modified stem: -st / -en / -en / -t / -en. F.1

## G. Past Perfect tense
G. Past Perfect tense G.1. When narrating a series of events, you sometimes need to emphasize that one event (A) comes before another (B). This might be the case, for example, if you happen to mention (B) first, and then need to clarify that (A) came before that; or if (B) only makes sense if (A) happened first: There were no more umbrellas at beach, since the other tourists had already taken them all. When you read (or hear) had already taken , you mentally place that action before the discovery that there were no more umbrellas , because of the verb had taken , as well as the adverbial element already . The verb had taken is in the Past Perfect tense. English speakers don’t use the Past Perfect very often in conversation, and neither do Germans. In most situations, the sequence of (A) and (B) is clear simply because (A) is mentioned first, followed by (B). But where more precision is needed, both German and English make use of the Past Perfect tense to establish the correct sequence of events: Es gab keine Sonnenschirme mehr, denn die anderen Touristen hatten sie alle schon genommen . There were no more umbrellas, since the other tourists had already taken them all. Wir waren nur zu dritt da, denn Jonas war nicht rechtzeitig aufgestanden . There were only three of us there, since Jonas had not gotten up on time. G.1
G.2. As you can see, and as you may have noticed in several of the Interaktionen in this section, the Past Perfect tense closely parallels the structure of the Perfect tense. It consists of an auxiliary verb in V 1 position — either haben or sein , depending on the main verb — and the past participle of that verb at the end of the clause ( V 2 ). The critical difference is that the auxiliary verbs are the Simple Past forms of haben and sein : hatte and war . nehmen: hatte … genommen had taken aufstehen: war … aufgestanden had gotten up [NB: English does the same thing, using had instead of have in forming the Past Perfect: had taken / had gotten up. ] G.2

## H. wurde / würde
H. wurde / würde H.1 Werden is a mixed verb, similar to bringen and denken , insofar as it forms the Simple Past by changing its stem and adding past-tense endings. But werden is slightly different from other mixed verbs, in that it deletes the -t- from the past tense endings used with mixed and weak verbs (brach t e/dach t est/sag t en) before affixing the ending to the modified stem: werden stem: wurd- ich wurde ( familiar ) du ( formal ) Sie wurdest wurden er/sie/es wurde wir wurden ( familiar ) ihr ( formal ) Sie wurdet wurden sie wurden A few examples: Abends wurde es am Strand ganz kühl. In the evenings it became/got quite cool on the beach. Warum wurdest du so böse? Why did you become/get so angry? Wir wurden gute Freunde. We became good friends. Plötzlich wurde mir alles klar. Suddenly everything became clear to me. H.1
H.2 würde While the subjunctive mood ( if I were you / if only we could / I would have done that / etc.) won’t be dealt with in detail until Chapter 15, you can form the most frequently used subjunctive word in German (würde) simply by adding an umlaut to wurde- and then using it just like a modal verb to mean would . würden would subjunctive stem: würd- ich würde ( familiar ) du ( formal ) Sie würdest würden er/sie/es würde wir würden ( familiar ) ihr ( formal ) Sie würdet würden sie würden Some examples: Was würdest du in dieser Situation machen? What would you do in this situation? Ich würde einfach warten. I would simply wait. Würden Sie mir bitte helfen? Would you please help me? Wir würden Ihnen helfen, wenn ... We would help you if … Notice in these examples how the use of würde + infinitive conforms to what you’ve learned about modal verbs in statements and questions: Statements: Ich würde … warten. ( Würde functions as V 1 and the infinitive moves to the end of the clause as V 2 .) »Wer-wie-was« questions: Was würdest du … machen? (The question word is the first element, followed by würde as V 1 , with V 2 at the end of the clause.) Verb-first questions: Würden Sie … helfen? ( Würde is the first element, and again, V 2 moves to the end of the clause.) There’s more to learn about würde , but the würde + infinitive combination can be used just about anytime you want to say I would [verb] in the present tense. H.2
H.3 The combination of würde and gern , followed with an infinitive at end of the clause, provides an easy way of saying I would like to [verb] — more or less synonymous with möchte . Diesen Sommer würde ich gern nach Europa reisen. This summer, I would like to travel to Europe. Und später würde ich auch gern einige Freunde in Australien besuchen. And later I’d also like to visit several friends in Australia. H.3
H.4 Würde gern or möchte? In case you’re wondering … You've learned that both möchte and würde gern mean would like . Are they interchangeable? Sometimes — but there are subtle differences in use and meaning. Use: Würde gern and möchte can both work with infinitives; but only möchte can take a direct object (noun or pronoun). In other words, if you want to say I would like to fly to Palma, using the verb fliegen, you can do so with either möchte or würde gern. But if you want to say I would like two tickets to Palma, you can only use möchte (or hätte gern , as you learned in Chapter 4). Meaning: Möchte is often used as a polite way of expressing what you would like, say, when you’re ordering in a restaurant, and is therefore closely associated with "present" desires. Würde gern + infinitive comes across as more speculative, and is therefore better suited to references about future actions, such as I would like to live (someday) in Berlin. But if you include adverbs in the sentence that clarify the time frame, such as jetzt (now) or eines Tages (someday), then möchte and würde gern are interchangeable. Compare: Ich möchte mit dir sprechen. I would like to speak with you. (implied: right now) Ich würde gern nach Afrika reisen. I would like to travel to Africa. (implied: someday) But: Ich möchte / würde jetzt gern mit dir sprechen. I would like to speak with you now. Ich möchte / würde gern eines Tages nach Afrika reisen. I would like to travel to Africa someday. H.4

## I. Genitive case
I. Genitive case I.1 The genitive case is often used with two nouns to show a relationship between them, one that is sometimes expressed in English with of _____. A few examples: the parents of my best friend , the promise of spring, or the color of your shirt . Sometimes the -’s construction is used instead, as in my best friend ’s parents . German uses the genitive case to cover both constructions. The forms of the genitive in German are so distinct that you should be able to infer them from a list of examples. I.3. Two more things about the genitive case: The rule you learned (in Chapter 4) pertaining to masculine »N-nouns« applies here as well. N-nouns in the genitive case add -(e)n, just as they do in the accusative and dative case. And because they take this ending, they do not add the genitive -(e)s that other masculine (and neuter) nouns take in the genitive. (Exception: Name) Nominative: Accusative: Dative: Genitive: der Junge ein Junge den Jungen einen Jungen dem Jungen einem Jungen des Jung en eines Jung en der Mensch ein Mensch den Menschen einen Menschen dem Menschen einem Menschen des Mensch en eines Mensch en der Name ein Name den Namen einen Namen dem Namen einem Namen des Name n s eines Name n s der Student ein Student den Studenten einen Studenten dem Studenten einem Studenten des Student en eines Student en While we’re on the topic of N-nouns: The neuter noun Herz (a core vocabulary word), behaves like an N-noun in the dative case, adding -en ; but, like Name , it adds -ens in the genitive case. Nominative: Accusative: Dative: Genitive: das Herz ein Herz das Herz ein Herz dem Herz en einem Herz en des Herz ens eines Herz ens As you've repeatedly seen by now, German genitive articles express the meaning of two words in English, the first of which is of. This means that a phrase in English such as my friend’s sofa has to be turned around to the sofa of my friend in order to express it properly in German: I’m buying my friend’s sofa. → I’m buying the sofa of my friend . → Ich kaufe das Sofa meines Freundes . If you try to express it the other way around, a German speaker will hear it as *of my friend sofa . You may encounter this formation in older texts, or in poetry, but it's not acceptable in normal speech. The only exception to this rule involves proper names, which function in German as they do in English — but without the apostrophe: Lisas Eltern Philipps Freunde If the name already ends with an -s (or s- like sound: -x, -z, etc.), an apostrophe is used in place of the -s: Fritz’ Kinder Marx' Vorstellung vom Kapital I.3

## J. Genitive prepositions
J. Genitive prepositions Just as certain German prepositions trigger the accusative case or the dative case, the prepositions you've learned the core vocabulary of this section trigger the genitive case. (Notice that the English meaning for these words usually includes the word of: in the face of / instead of / because of / on the basis of / etc.) angesichts in the face of / in view of aufgrund on the basis of, due to außerhalb outside of innerhalb within, inside of statt instead of trotz in spite of während during, in the course of wegen because of Angesichts der politischen Lage ... In view of / Considering the political situation ... Wir wohnen außerhalb der Stadt. We live outside of the city. Was machst du während deines Urlaubs? What are you doing during your vacation? Trotz ihres Jobs hat sie immer Zeit für Freunde. In spite of her job, she always has time for friends. Aufgrund des schlechten Wetters findet das Open-Air-Concert nicht statt.
