--- Thema 8.1: Stadtbild(er) ---

## A. Subject-verb inversion
A. Subject-verb inversion A.1 Whether you've noticed them or not, you’ve encountered literally hundreds of examples of a grammatical structure in German that differs markedly from English: Manchmal essen wir bei McDonald’s. Im 19. Jahrhundert ist Leipzig viel größer geworden. Auf Instagram gibt es viele Storys. Diese Filme nennt man »Heimatfilme«. In der DDR waren die alten Autos teurer als neue Modelle. Den ganzen Sommer müssen die Fans auf den ersten Spieltag warten. What's the difference? Word order. Notice how the elements in the first German sentence are sequenced as compared with its English translation: Manchmal essen wir bei McDonald’s. Sometimes we eat at McDonald’s. Both sentences begin with a time element: Manchmal ... / Sometimes ... , but whereas English continues with the usual subject/verb pattern ( ... we eat ... ), German inverts that pattern and places the subject after the verb: ... essen wir ... . Looking at the other examples, you can see that this holds true regardless of whether the initial element is a one-word element ( Manchmal … ), two words ( Auf Instagram … / Diese Filme … ), or even longer ( Im 19. Jahrhundert … / In der DDR … / Den ganzen Sommer … ). You’ll also notice that these elements have various functions — some are adverbial modifiers of time and place, and one of them is a direct object ( Diese Filme ). The important difference here is that German allows only one such element to appear before the conjugated verb, in what is often called the “front field” of the sentence. And if that element is not the subject, then the subject must come after the verb. To put it another way: The conjugated verb must always be the second element. This rule is easy enough to articulate — but it turns out to be relatively difficult to put into practice for learners whose native language doesn’t follow this pattern. English speakers are so used to stating the subject before the verb, no matter how much information precedes it, that they instinctively do so in German as well. (And even non-native speakers of English tend to stumble over this when they learn German.) A.1
A.2 Coordinating conjunctions This rule about verb placement pertains to units of ideas known as independent clauses. So far, each of the examples and exercises in this section has made use of a single independent clause: Manchmal essen wir bei McDonald’s. Auf Instagram gibt es viele Storys. In der DDR waren die alten Autos teurer als neue Modelle. But independent clauses are routinely joined together by way of words such as und, aber, denn and oder, which are known as coordinating conjunctions. These conjunctions do not affect the word order within the clauses they connect; they simply tie them together. In other words, an independent clause following und, aber, denn and oder begins with its own first element, not the conjugated verb: Manchmal essen wir bei McDonald’s, und dann bestelle ich mir einen Big Mac mit Pommes. Auf Instagram gibt es viele Storys, aber natürlich sind nicht alle ganz echt. In der DDR waren die alten Autos teurer als neue Modelle, denn man musste so lang auf die neuen Modelle warten. A.2

--- Thema 8.2: Innenstadt ---

## C. Infinitive clauses
C. Infinitive clauses C.1 German, like English, organizes words into various kinds of information units, or clauses: independent clauses, infinitive clauses, dependent clauses and relative clauses. You've encountered all four types, but the focus until now has been on independent clauses, i.e., groups of words that include (at least) a subject and a conjugated verb; that express a complete thought, with no need (strictly speaking) of any other clauses; and that follow the conjugated-verb-second rule presented in Thema 1 of this chapter. This section focuses on a second clause type, known as infinitive clauses. To show how these work we'll begin with a short dialogue in English: Roommate 1: Do you really have time to play video games? Roommate 2: I decided to take a break. Roommate 1: But you have a big test tomorrow ... Roommate 2: And I have absolutely no desire to get ready for it. Go away. All of these sentences contain independent clauses, either in the form of a question or a statement: Do you have time …? I decided … You have a big test ... I have absolutely no desire … What follows in three of them is a clause that begins with a verb infinitive (to play, to take, to get ready), followed by various other elements, such as direct objects and modifiers: … to play video games … to take a break … to get ready for it Now compare these clauses with their German equivalents: …, Videospiele zu spielen . …, eine Pause zu machen . …, mich darauf vorzubereiten° . Notice that there's no subject in these clauses — either in German or English — which is why the verb remains in infinitive form. Notice too how to and zu appear separately just before the infinitive. (In clauses containing a separable-prefix verb, such as vor•bereiten, the zu is inserted between the prefix and the main verb to form one word: vorzubereiten ). But there is an important difference between the two languages: English places the infinitive phrase at the beginning of the clause ( to play video games ), but in German it appears at the very end (Videospiele zu spielen ). C.1
C.2 There are a number of commonly used independent clauses that lead naturally into infinitive clauses: Ich habe (keine) Zeit, … Ich habe keine Zeit, alle meine Aufgaben zu machen. Sie hat manchmal Zeit, nachmittags joggen zu gehen.* Haben wir genug Zeit, in die Stadt zu fahren? Es macht mir (keinen) Spaß, … Es macht mir Spaß, stundenlang im Café zu sitzen und Kaffee zu trinken. Mir hat es keinen Spaß gemacht, die Prüfung in Bio zu schreiben. Macht es dir Spaß, shoppen zu gehen?* Ich habe mich entschieden, ... Wir haben uns entschieden, in der Pause nach Mallorca zu fliegen. Sie hat sich entschieden, Chemie zu studieren. Ich finde es (nicht) wichtig / interessant / einfach, … Ich finde es nicht einfach, diese Konzepte zu verstehen. Findest du es interessant, Filme in einer Fremdsprache zu sehen? Er findet es manchmal schwer, die ganze Wahrheit zu sagen. Ich beginne, … / Ich fange an, … Ich muss anfangen, mehr zu schlafen und gesünder° zu essen. Sie hat letztes Jahr begonnen, sich sehr für Kunstgeschichte zu interessieren. Wann können wir beginnen, darüber zu diskutieren? Es gibt keinen Grund, … / Es gibt gute Gründe, … Es gibt keinen Grund, so etwas zu sagen. Es gibt viele gute Gründe, Fremdsprachen zu lernen. * Compound verbs such as joggen gehen and shoppen gehen insert zu into the verb phrase as a separate word: … joggen zu gehen. C.2
C.3 In English, some modal verb structures include to , just like the infinitives in infinitive clauses: Her family was able to travel to Switzerland every year. This summer I finally have time to travel to Switzerland. I want to stay in touch with my friends back home. Sometimes it's difficult to stay in touch with them. German differs from English in this respect. Modal structures absorb zu into the modal verb so that it disappears, whereas it always appears in infinitive clauses. Compare: Ihre Familie konnte jedes Jahr in die Schweiz reisen . Diesen Sommer habe ich endlich Zeit, in die Schweiz zu reisen . Ich will mit meinen Freunden zu Hause in Kontakt bleiben . Manchmal ist es ganz schwer, mit ihnen in Kontakt zu bleiben . C.3
C.4 A word on punctuation: You may have noticed that many German sentences with infinitive clauses contain a comma where English has none. German routinely uses commas to separate the various kinds of clauses from each other — something that can be very helpful in comprehending long sentences. In fact, the only time you don't use a comma to set off an infinitive clause is when the infinitive stands by itself after an independent clause, with no intervening objects or modifiers. Compare: Es macht mir Spaß , langsam zu lesen. Es macht mir Spaß , sehr alte Bücher zu lesen. Es macht mir Spaß zu lesen. (the infinitive stands alone → no comma) Hast du heute Zeit , mit mir zu essen? Hast du heute Zeit , an deinem Projekt zu arbeiten? Hast du heute Zeit zu essen? (the infinitive stands alone → no comma) C.4

In this review, however, we want to take all three cases into account, as well as all the options for article usage, by looking at strong vs. weak endings. ## F. Review: Present tense
F. Review: Present tense While you most likely feel comfortable using the present tense by now, it's important to keep in mind the ways in which English and German differ in how they use it. To illustrate this we’ll use two familiar verbs: arbeiten to work schreiben to write F.1 Ask yourself how the specific forms used in the following sentences (e.g. work / works / working ) affect the meanings of the verbs in context: She usually works in Firestone or in her room. She’s working on her thesis. She's writing it right now, in fact. Does she work alone or with friends? Does she always write multiple drafts? Is she writing it at the moment? She’ll work more on it later. She’ll write it over the weekend. She’s been working on it since Monday. She’s been writing for days. In each of these sentences, work and write combine with various auxiliaries and suffixes to express different nuances of meaning: Statements: general: She works … process: She is work ing … (emphasis: right now / in process ) Questions: general: Does she always write …? process: Is she work ing …? Future tense: She’ ll write … Present perfect: She’ s been work ing … Notice that the only context in which you find an English present-tense verb stripped of all auxiliaries involves statements expressing general validity: She works on her thesis at night. She usually writes from 11pm to 2am. English modal verbs are an exception to this last rule, in that they routinely appear without auxiliaries: She can work on it tonight. May I ask you a question? Must the paper be handed in tomorrow? But not always: Does the paper have to be handed in tomorrow? (Good luck trying to explain this to a non-native speaker.) F.1
F.2 By contrast, German simply uses the present-tense form of the verb in all of these contexts: Statements expressing both general validity and process: Sie arbeitet… Sie schreibt… She is working … / She works … She is writing … / She writes … Questions expressing both general validity and process: Arbeitet sie…? Schreibt sie…? Does she work …? / Is she working …? Does she write …? / Is she writing …? Statements and questions referring to the future (as long as future time is implied by the context): Sie arbeitet später daran. Sie schreibt es am Wochenende. She’ll work on it later. She’ll write it over the weekend. (German does, in fact, have a future tense form, which will be introduced in a later chapter; but whenever the context clearly points to future time, German speakers prefer to use the present tense.) Present perfect tense (i.e., referring to something that began in the past but is ongoing in the present): Sie arbeitet seit Montag. Sie schreibt seit Tagen. She’s been working since Monday. She’s been writing for days. Notice that the action's starting point (since Monday) as well as its duration (for days), is expressed with seit , followed by the dative case. F.2

## H. Review: Modal verbs
H. Review: Modal verbs H.1 Modal verbs convey a “mode” (an attitude or posture) regarding the main verb in the clause, allowing you to communicate not merely that someone is doing something, but that the person must do it, or wants to do it, or can do it. There are six modal verbs in German, one of which frequently appears in its subjunctive form, which is shown here in brackets: dürfen to be allowed to; may (permission) können to be able to; can mögen to like [ möchten would like to ] müssen to have to; must sollen to be supposed to; should wollen to want to Points to keep in mind when using modal verbs: Word order : Modal verbs function as V 1 in the “sentence bracket,” meaning that they appear immediately after the first element of an independent clause. The infinitive linked to the modal verb functions as V 2 , appearing at the end of the clause: Heute Abend können wir vielleicht zusammen essen . Conjugation : Modal verbs have irregular conjugations. All but one ( sollen ) undergo a change in the stem vowel in the forms for ich/du/er/sie/es ; and the forms for ich as well as er/sie/es are identical — just the stem, with no ending. Meaning : können/müssen/wollen correspond closely with their English equivalents can/must/want; but dürfen, sollen and mögen don’t map as easily onto English meanings, and you should review the explanations and examples in Chapter 6 to be sure you know how to use them. Negation : Most modal verbs are negated simply by adding nicht , so that können + nicht means cannot, wollen + nicht means doesn’t want to, and so on. But müssen is a special case: Wir müssen nicht means We don't have to (i.e., not required); the stronger meaning must not (i.e., not permissible) is expressed by dürfen + nicht . H.1

## I. Review: Negation
I. Review: Negation There are two kinds of negation in German: negation using kein- negation using nicht Both kinds have been discussed from Chapter 3 on; but now that you’ve been introduced to the concept of non-specific vs. specific nouns (Chapter 5), we can discuss all the applications of kein- and nicht using this framework. I.1 First, a reminder of what is meant by non-specific vs. specific nouns: Non-specific nouns refer to general entities ( Essen , Geld , Zeit ), including plural nouns with no article ( Geschäfte , Kinder ) and non-specific examples ( eine Stadt, ein Kurs ). Specific nouns , by contrast, have a focused reference, indicated by the use of definite articles ( das Essen, das Geld, die Zeit ), possessive adjectives ( seine Geschäfte, unsere Kinder ) or a specifying word ( dieses Essen ). Pronouns are also considered specific, since they refer by definition to something that has already been mentioned. Compare: non-specific specific Geld dieses Geld / mein Geld / das Geld von … Kinder die Kinder von … / ihre Kinder / diese Kinder eine Stadt die Stadt / diese Stadt / unsere Stadt I.1
I.2 Negating with kein- To negate a non-specific noun, use kein- . This applies to any non-specific noun used as a predicate noun (nominative), a direct object (accusative) or an indirect object (dative). The endings you use with kein- will depend on the case, gender, and number of the noun following it. Original: Negated: Nom. Lisa ist eine Freundin von mir. Aber sie ist keine Freundin von Daniel. Acc. Im Süden von Deutschland gibt es Berge . Im Norden gibt es keine Berge . Acc. Er braucht ein Auto . Aber er kauft kein Auto . Dat. Die Orange-Key- Gästeführer° zeigen Besuchern den Campus. Aber sie zeigen keinen Besuchern die Zimmer im Forbes-Annex. I.2
I.3 Negating with nicht To negate everything else — specific nouns, pronouns, verbs, adverbs (including gern ), adjectives, and prepositional phrases — use nicht. The position of nicht depends on which of these elements is being negated, and in most situations this is determined as follows: specific nouns pronouns verbs ( V 1 ) nicht adverbs adjectives prepositional phrases In these examples, the elements to be negated are underlined in order to highlight their relation to nicht in the negated version: Original: Negated: Sie kennt meine Freunde . Sie kennt meine Freunde nicht . Er hilft mir . Er hilft mir nicht . Du bleibst . Du bleibst nicht . Er fährt schnell . Er fährt nicht schnell . Ihr Zimmer ist sehr groß . Ihr Zimmer ist nicht sehr groß . Hast du auf mich gewartet? Hast du nicht auf mich gewartet? But: If you really want to emphasize the negation of a specific noun or pronoun — and particularly if you want to follow up with corrective information — then you place nicht before the element to be negated, regardless of what it is, and you emphasize the negated element with intonation as well. The subsequent correction is then introduced with sondern or aber , and can also be emphasized with intonation. Structurally, the phrase looks like this: nicht X , sondern Y (or) nicht X , aber … Here are a few examples – with the italicized elements showing intonational emphasis: Original: Negated: Sie kennt meine Freunde. Sie kennt nicht meine Freunde , sondern meine Eltern . Er hilft mir. Er hilft nicht mir , sondern meinem Bruder . Sie hat mir eine E-Mail geschickt. Sie hat mir nicht eine E-Mail geschickt, sondern eine SMS . I.3
I.4 That’s all well and good, you’re thinking to yourself, as long as the number of elements is kept to a minimum and the sentence is in the present tense. But what happens to the rules governing kein- and nicht when you complicate things with “sentence brackets,” i.e., when you introduce modal verbs, auxiliaries, and past participles? Nothing, actually. In sentences with a V 1 – V 2 structure, nicht and kein- function as they do when there’s only one verb. Nicht precedes or follows the negated element in the middle field, as needed, and kein- still precedes non-specific nouns. Notice how this works in an example with a specific noun and nicht : Original: Er versteht die Frage . Negated: Er versteht die Frage nicht . Negated with modal verb: Er kann die Frage nicht verstehen Negated in the perfect tense: Er hat die Frage nicht verstanden. Various other elements shift their position, but die Frage nicht remains unchanged. I.4

## J. Review: Perfect tense
J. Review: Perfect tense J.1 Form and meaning eben° The Perfect tense consists of an auxiliary (either haben or sein , depending on the verb) and a past participle. In an independent clause, the auxiliary functions as V 1 , and the past participle functions as V 2 , appearing at the end of the clause. Hast du eben was gesagt ? Did you just say something? Ich habe deine Frage nicht verstanden . I didn’t understand your question. Sie hat uns alles erklärt . She explained everything to us. Er hat uns zum Fest eingeladen . He invited us to the celebration. Der Hund ist draußen geblieben . The dog stayed outside. The Perfect tense refers to activities in the past that are “perfected,” i.e., finished and no longer taking place — regardless of when they began or how long they lasted. (Remember: If an activity is ongoing in the speaker’s “present,” German signals this by using the present tense, with a prepositional phrase beginning with seit to indicate either the starting point or the duration of the activity.) The Perfect tense translates into English either with an auxiliary form, or with a one-word past-tense form: ist … gegangen has gone / went hat … gedacht has thought / thought hat … diskutiert has discussed / discussed J.1
J.2 The challenge for most learners lies not in understanding the structure or meaning of the Perfect tense, but in learning the past participle for each verb, and then remembering which verbs use sein and which use haben as their auxiliary.
