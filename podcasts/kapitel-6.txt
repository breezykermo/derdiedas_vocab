Audience: beginners (first year of college German) working through the der|die|das German curriculum. They have just
finished Kapitel 6 ("Heimat") and have studied every earlier chapter.

Format: a 15-20 minute conversation between two hosts:
- EXPERT: a fluent, near-native German speaker. Patient, warm, precise.
- LEARNER: a student at exactly this point in the course (end of Kapitel 6). Keen and
  chatty, but makes the kinds of mistakes real learners make at this stage.
The episode is a real conversation about the chapter's topics, not a lecture. The
LEARNER keeps trying to say things in German; the EXPERT keeps the conversation going
and corrects the LEARNER's German as they go.

How the corrections work (the core of the episode):
- The LEARNER makes roughly 12-20 mistakes over the episode. Most should target the
  Kapitel 6 grammar points below; the rest should come from earlier chapters (wrong
  article/gender, verb endings, plural forms, word order, a vocabulary mix-up).
  Mistakes must be plausible for this level, never random nonsense.
- Every mistake gets corrected — none slip past. The EXPERT either recasts it ("Ah, du
  meinst: ...") or stops briefly to explain the rule in one or two sentences, then gives
  a correct model sentence.
- The LEARNER then repeats the corrected sentence and, where it fits, makes a new
  sentence using the same rule correctly, so the listener hears the right form at least
  twice for every time they hear the wrong one.
- Make the mistake and its correction unmistakable to a listener: the EXPERT should say
  clearly what was wrong (e.g. "Nicht 'der Stadt' — 'die Stadt', Stadt ist feminin").
- Now and then the EXPERT asks the listener directly: "Did you catch the mistake?
  Pause and fix it yourself" — then gives the answer.
- Corrections are kind and encouraging; the LEARNER sometimes self-corrects or
  asks "Warum?" to prompt an explanation.

Language: Speak mostly German (about 70%) in short, simple sentences. Use English for grammar explanations and to gloss new or tricky words, but let familiar German sentences stand without translation.
That level applies to the conversation as a whole; the EXPERT's grammar explanations
may be in English where the level calls for it.

Hard constraints:
- German vocabulary: use ONLY words from the vocabulary lists in this source (this
  chapter's list plus the earlier-chapter list), along with names, numbers, obvious
  international cognates (Internet, Party, Computer ...) and the small function words
  (articles, pronouns, und, oder, aber, in, auf ...). If you need a word that is not
  listed, say it once and immediately explain it.
- German grammar: correct sentences use ONLY structures from Kapitel 1-6 (see the
  grammar sections below). Don't introduce tenses, cases or constructions taught later
  in the course, and don't "correct" the LEARNER into a structure they haven't learned.
- Always say nouns with their article (der/die/das) the first time they come up, and
  give the plural when it is listed.

Suggested shape:
1. Quick welcome: the EXPERT introduces the LEARNER and the chapter theme "Heimat"
   (Themen listed below).
2. Conversation about the Themen, with many of this chapter's NEW words. The LEARNER
   talks about their own life and opinions; mistakes and corrections happen naturally.
3. Grammar focus: the EXPERT sets up situations that force each main Kapitel 6
   grammar point; the LEARNER struggles, is corrected, and gets it right.
4. Review: the conversation drifts to topics from earlier chapters so older words and
   grammar come back (with a few old mistakes corrected again).
5. Wrap-up: the LEARNER sums up what they learned — the trickiest corrections of the
   episode and the key new words — and the EXPERT confirms.

--- Thema 6.1: Was ist »Heimat«? ---

## A. Review: Constructions using multiple verbs
A. Review: Constructions using multiple verbs You're already familiar with several grammatical structures that connect two verbs within a single clause: Ich gehe … joggen . In Chapter 3 you saw how gehen can be combined with an infinitive to express the idea of going off to do something — whether it’s going off to exercise, buy shoes, or get a cup of coffee. Ich gehe jeden Morgen 5 Kilometer joggen . Er geht manchmal mit seiner Freundin Schuhe kaufen . Um vier Uhr gehen wir Kaffee trinken . Ich habe … gegessen . In Chapter 5 you learned how an auxiliary verb ( haben or sein ) can be paired with a past participle to form the Perfect tense: Ich bin jeden Morgen 5 Kilometer gelaufen . Er hat manchmal mit seiner Freundin neue Schuhe gekauft . Um vier Uhr haben wir Kaffee getrunken . Both are obviously similar in structure: The first verb (we’ll call it V 1 ) appears toward the beginning of the clause and conjugates with the subject, while the second verb ( V 2 ) appears at the end of the clause, with any number of words inserted between it and V 1 . This structure is used so frequently in German that it has its own name: the sentence bracket ( die Satzklammer ). The two verbs “bracket” whatever information comes between them — which can include modifiers, objects, indirect objects, and sometimes even the sentence subject. Remember: While English also uses a two-verb structure in the phrase to like to do something — She likes to dance. He likes to read novels. — German usually expresses this idea with the adverb gern and a verb: Sie tanzt gern . Er liest gern Romane. A

## B. Modal verbs: können / wollen / müssen
B. Modal verbs: können / wollen / müssen B.1 German also uses this “sentence bracket” with verbs such as can, must, and want to , which are commonly referred to as modal verbs. To illustrate how this works, we'll start with a simple sentence: Ich spreche Deutsch. Adding modal verbs to this sentence allows the speaker to convey the idea of an ability (I can speak German), a desire (I want to speak German), or a necessity (I must speak German): Ich kann Deutsch sprechen . Ich will Deutsch sprechen . Ich muss Deutsch sprechen . Notice the structural difference here between English and German: In English, the modal verb and the infinitive are grouped together; but in German, the modal verb functions as V 1 while the infinitive ( V 2 ) comes at the very end, just like a past participle in the Perfect tense. (NB: Separable-prefix verbs remain one word when they function as V 2 verbs in modal constructions.) B.1
B.2 Können | wollen | müssen Here are the conjugated forms for the three modal verbs shown above, in the present tense: können can, to be able to singular plural first-person ich kann wir können second-person du kannst [familiar] Sie können [formal] ihr könnt [familiar] Sie können [formal] third-person er /der Wagen kann sie/die Gruppe kann es/das Modell kann sie/die Autos können wollen to want to singular plural first-person ich will wir wollen second-person du willst [familiar] Sie wollen [formal] ihr wollt [familiar] Sie wollen [formal] third-person er /der Mann will sie/die Frau will es/das Kind will sie/die Leute wollen NB: Ich will does not mean I will , but rather I want to . (Granted, I want to usually connotes future activity, but wollen expresses present desire and intention, not the future tense.) müssen must, to have to singular plural first-person ich muss wir müssen second-person du musst [familiar] Sie müssen [formal] ihr müsst [familiar] Sie müssen [formal] third-person er /der Student muss sie/die Studentin muss es/das Problem muss sie/die Aufgaben müssen B.2
B.3 Questions using modal verbs follow the formats you’ve already learned, with the modal infinitive serving as V 1 : Verb-first questions: V 1 + subject … V 2 ? Willst du nach Hause gehen? Kannst du schwimmen? »Wer-wie-was« questions: Question word + V 1 + subject … V 2 ? Wann willst du nach Hause gehen? Wie weit kannst du schwimmen? Tag questions: [ … ], nicht wahr? / [ … ], oder? Du willst nach Hause gehen, oder? Du kannst schwimmen, nicht wahr? B.3

## C. Modal verbs: dürfen | sollen
C. Modal verbs: dürfen | sollen These two modal verbs function structurally just like können, müssen and wollen, but require special attention to their meanings. C.1 Dürfen dürfen to be allowed to; may (permission) singular plural first-person ich darf wir dürfen second-person du darfst [familiar] Sie dürfen [formal] ihr dürft [familiar] Sie dürfen [formal] third-person er/sein Bruder darf sie/seine Schwester darf es/das Kind darf sie/die Leute dürfen Notice that this verb means to be allowed , rather than to allow – i.e., it denotes permission granted to the sentence subject, rather than the subject’s granting of permission. (To express I allow you to do that , German uses the verb lassen , which will be introduced later.) Some examples: Seniors dürfen drei Kurse im letzten Semester machen. Seniors are allowed to take three courses in the last semester. Darf ich fragen, was eine »P-rade« ist? May I ask what a “P-rade” is? Du darfst keine Kerzen° in deinem Zimmer haben! You’re not allowed to have any candles in your room! Darf man auf Cannon Green Croquet spielen? Are you allowed to play croquet on Cannon Green? C.1
C.2 Sollen sollen to be supposed to, to be expected to; should singular plural first-person ich soll wir sollen second-person du sollst [familiar] Sie sollen [formal] ihr sollt [familiar] Sie sollen [formal] third-person er /der Student soll sie/die Uni soll es/das Problem soll sie/die Professoren sollen Where dürfen clarifies a potential ambiguity in English, sollen introduces one. Consider the difference in English between should and to be supposed to: I should do that. I’m supposed to/expected to do that. Not quite the same, are they? But sollen is used in colloquial German to express both of them: Ich soll das machen is an acceptable translation of both sentences above. To make the distinction between I’m expected to and I think I should , German speakers frequently make use of a subjunctive form of sollen (which will be discussed in detail later) to convey a personal sense of responsibility on the speaker’s part, as opposed to external pressure: Ich sollte das machen. Here’s a rule of thumb: If your intention is to express the idea that someone said I’m supposed to do this, then use soll . If the meaning is simply I think this is what I should do, you can use either soll or sollte . The forms for sollte are similar to soll : first- and third-person singular are sollte , and the rest take the expected endings: solltest / sollten / solltet. C.2

## D. Mögen / ich möchte …
D. Mögen / ich möchte … D.1 Mögen You already know that mögen means to like , as in Ich mag diese Musik . Now that you’ve encountered other modal verbs, you can see that the vowel change from mögen to mag as well as the lack of a verb ending for ich mag / er mag / sie mag are typical for modal verbs: mögen to like singular plural first-person ich mag wir mögen second-person du magst [familiar] Sie mögen [formal] ihr mögt [familiar] Sie mögen [formal] third-person er /der Junge mag sie/die Frau mag es/das Kind mag sie/die Eltern mögen D.1
D.2 Möchte When you want to say I like [X], you can use mögen (to like) or its subjunctive form möchte (would like) by itself, with a direct object. Möchte is also used frequently in V 1 -V 2 structures to express the meaning would like to, with an infinitive at the end of the clause. Even though the subjunctive mood hasn't been introduced yet, möchte is used so frequently that you should learn its forms now: möcht- would like (to) singular plural first-person ich möchte wir möchten second-person du möchtest [familiar] Sie möchten [formal] ihr möchtet [familiar] Sie möchten [formal] third-person er /der Junge möchte sie /die Frau möchte es /das Kind möchte sie/die Eltern möchten A few examples: Ich möchte eine Katze. Was möchtest du heute machen ? Möchten Sie eine Tasse Kaffee? Er möchte wohl etwas länger schlafen . I would like a cat. What would you like to do today? Would you like a cup of coffee? He probably would like to sleep a little longer. D.2
D.3 A bit of etiquette concerning mögen / möchte / wollen: All three of these modals can stand alone with a direct object: Ich mag diese Pizza. Ich möchte ein Stück Pizza. Ich will ein Stück Pizza. I like this pizza. I’d like a piece of pizza. I want a piece of pizza. While all of these are grammatically correct, the last one ( Ich will …) can come across as quite blunt in situations involving requests — a bit like someone who insists I want that! When requesting something (say, in a restaurant), use möchte or (as you learned in chapter 4) hätte gern : Ich möchte noch eine Tasse Kaffee, bitte! Ich hätte gern ein Stück Apfeltorte. Wollen is perfectly acceptable, however, when it’s used in conjunction with another verb to express what you want to do , as is möchten , if you’d prefer to say what you’d like to do. D.3

## E. Modal verbs: Negation
E. Modal verbs: Negation E.1 Negating a sentence with a modal verb is just like negating any other sentence — except that the negation is integrated into a V 1 -V 2 structure. A brief review of the rules for negation you've learned so far: To say what something is not , with verbs such as sein and werden: if the negation uses not a … or no , use kein(e) in place of ein(e): Das ist kein Wohnheim. That’s not a dorm./ That's no dorm. if the negation only requires not in English, use nicht : Das ist nicht mein Wohnheim. That’s not my dorm. To negate a verb, i.e., to say that an action or state is not taking place: use nicht after the conjugated verb: Ich arbeite nicht . I’m not working. Wir gehen nicht tanzen. We’re not going dancing. To negate direct and indirect objects: with non-specific objects (preceded by ein or no article), use kein-: Ich habe keine Zeit . I don’t have any time. Die Uni gibt keinen Studierenden einen eigenen Parkplatz. The university doesn’t give any students their own parking space. with specific objects (nouns preceded by definite articles, possessive adjectives, or pronouns), use nicht after the object: Ich verlasse meine Heimat nicht . I’m not leaving my home. Du verstehst mich nicht . You don’t understand me. E.1
E.2 When modal verbs are added to sentences like these, the negating segments remain the same. Compare: non-modal: Die Uni baut kein neues Wohnheim . modal: Die Uni möchte kein neues Wohnheim bauen . non-modal: Meine Freunde bleiben nicht fürs ganze Wochenende . modal: Meine Freunde wollen nicht fürs ganze Wochenende bleiben . E.2
E.3 1. When modal verbs themselves are negated with nicht , the meaning usually corresponds to the equivalent modal in English + not: ich kann nicht … I’m not able to… / I cannot … ich darf nicht … I’m not allowed to … / I may not … (permission) ich will nicht … I don’t want to … ich möchte nicht … I wouldn’t like to… ich soll(te) nicht … I’m not supposed to … / (I shouldn’t …) 2. There is one exception: müssen + nicht Ich muss nicht … I don’t have to… Recall that müssen can mean either must or have to, which in English are virtually synonymous. But the English phrases that result from adding not to these words — must not / don’t have to — are clearly not synonymous. The phrase müssen + nicht expresses only the latter of these meanings, i.e., an absence of necessity, rather than a prohibition: Du musst das nicht machen. You don't have to do that. So how do you say must not ? Since this phrase is closely related in meaning to not allowed to , German uses dürfen + nicht here: Du darfst das nicht machen! You must not do that! / You aren't allowed to do that! E.3

## F. Modal verbs: Past tense
F. Modal verbs: Past tense F.1 Using modal verbs in the past tense is considerably easier in German than in English. Just think about how you would express the following present-tense sentences in the past, using English past-tense modal verbs: I want to sleep in today. Yesterday, I ... I must take a break. Two hours ago, I ... I should finish it in two hours. Yesterday, I ... I can do that quickly. Last week, I ... You see the problem? For want there's wanted , but there’s no *musted for must , and no *shoulded for should . So instead you take their synonyms have to and am supposed to , and put those in the past tense: had to and was supposed to . And even though can has the past tense form could, that’s the same form as the present-tense subjunctive (as in We could do that right now ), so to be unambiguous you need to say was able to . German, on the other hand, has one form for each modal verb, based on a past tense stem (ending in -t ) to which the following endings are added: singular plural first-person ich -e wir -en second-person du [familiar] Sie [formal] -est -en ihr [familiar] Sie [formal] -et -en third-person er/sie/es -e sie -en F.1
F.2 The past tense forms for the modal verbs — including the past tense forms for mögen* , in its meaning to like — are as follows: dürfen können müssen mögen sollen wollen stem: durft- konnt- musst- mocht- sollt- wollt- ich durfte konnte musste mochte sollte wollte du Sie durftest durften konntest konnten musstest mussten mochtest mochten solltest sollten wolltest wollten er/sie/es durfte konnte musste mochte sollte wollte wir durften konnten mussten mochten sollten wollten ihr Sie durftet durften konntet konnten musstet mussten mochtet mochten solltet sollten wolltet wollten sie durften konnten mussten mochten sollten wollten * The subjunctive form möchte exists only in the present tense, just as I would like to has no past tense form in English. To convey I would like to in the past, most people say I wanted to – and German speakers similarly use wollte. F.2
F.3 All of the forms shown in F.2 function as V 1 , while the second verb ( V 2 ) remains an infinitive, as it does in English: Ich kann nicht schlafen . I can’t sleep. / I’m not able to sleep. Ich konnte nicht schlafen . I couldn’t sleep. / I wasn’t able to sleep. Er will seine Familie besuchen . He wants to visit his family. Er wollte seine Familie besuchen . He wanted to visit his family. Notice: the “pastness” of the sentence is expressed by altering the modal verb, not the second verb ( V 2 ): Ich durfte es nicht kaufen . I wasn’t allowed to buy it. Wir mussten sprechen . We had to speak . Sie wollte zu Hause bleiben . She wanted to stay home. Warum konntest du nicht gehen ? Why weren’t you able to go ? F.3

--- Thema 6.4: Heimweh ---

## G. How to say: “I know…”
G. How to say: “I know…” Like many other European languages, German distinguishes between knowing in the sense of being acquainted with, and knowing in the sense of possessing information. This section explains how the verbs kennen and wissen address this distinction in meaning, as well as their respective forms and grammatical contexts. G.1 Kennen You’ve already learned the verb kennen , which denotes acquaintance with a person, a place, or an abstraction, concept, or idea. Kennen conjugates as a regular verb, with no alterations in the stem or its endings, and it always appears with an accusative direct object. Kennst du meinen Freund Marco? Do you know my friend Marco? Sie kennt ihre Heimatstadt wie ihre Westentasche. She knows her hometown like the back of her hand. (lit.: “like her vest pocket”) Wir kennen diese Probleme nur zu gut. We are acquainted with these problems only too well. In spoken German you’ll also hear kennen used to indicate knowledge of discrete bits of information, such as addresses, telephone numbers and names. Kennst du seinen Namen? Do you know his name? Leider kenne ich seine Adresse nicht. Unfortunately, I don’t know his address. Hoffentlich kennt er meine Handynummer. I hope he knows my cell phone number. The past participle of kennen is gekannt. Wie viele Leute hast du auf der Party gekannt ? How many people did you know at the party? G.1
G.2 Wissen To express the idea of knowing information , German uses the verb wissen . Four important points to keep in mind: 1. Wissen conjugates like a modal verb. When used with ich, du, and er/sie/es, the stem changes from wiss- to weiß-, and there are no endings for the first- and third-person singular forms. wissen to know (information) singular plural first-person ich weiß wir wissen second-person du weißt [familiar] Sie wissen [formal] ihr wisst [familiar] Sie wissen [formal] third-person er/sein Bruder weiß sie/seine Schwester weiß es/das Kind weiß sie/die Leute wissen 2. Wissen can stand alone, as in the simple statement I know , but it often appears with a direct object: Ich weiß . I know. Weißt du das? / Weißt du das nicht? Do you know that? / Don’t you know that? Wir wissen alles! We know everything! Das weiß ich [schon]. I [already] know that. Das weiß ich nicht. I don’t know that. As the last two examples above illustrate, the direct object das can appear as the first element in the sentence, with the subject appearing after wissen . In colloquial speech this happens so frequently, in fact, that many German speakers leave it out altogether: Ja, weiß ich (similar in tone to yeah, I know ) or Weiß ich nicht (similar in tone to dunno ). 3. Wissen is always used to express know in sentences that contain dependent clauses, like these: We don’t know if it’s going to rain or not . Did you know that she’s a movie star ? I know what you did last summer . Dependent clauses will be discussed in Chapter 10 — but you should be aware even at this point that wissen is frequently used to “set up” this clause structure, since you will see it used frequently in texts of all kinds (and in anticipation of that, in one of the exercises below). 4. Since wissen has to do with knowing information , it is used (along with kennen, as mentioned in G.1 ) to talk about knowing names, addresses and telephone numbers. This is the only context in which wissen and kennen are interchangeable. Weißt/Kennst du ihren Namen? Do you know her name? Leider weiß/kenne ich ihre Adresse nicht. Unfortunately, I don’t know her address. Hoffentlich weiß/kennt sie meine Handynummer. I hope she knows my cell phone number. G.2

## H. Stand-alone modal verbs
H. Stand-alone modal verbs H.1 In certain situations, both English and German allow modal verbs to stand alone, i.e., to function as the only verb in the sentence, with no infinitive in tow: Where the infinitive would be haben and the modal verb expresses desire : Ich möchte eine Tasse Kaffee [haben]. I’d like [to have] a cup of coffee. Wir wollen mehr Zeit [haben]. We want [to have] more time. Where the infinitive is mentioned in a previous utterance and therefore understood: Gehst du jetzt nach Hause? — Ja, ich muss . Are you going home now? — Yes, I have to. H.1
H.2 But German allows modal verbs to stand alone in several contexts where English requires an accompanying infinitive: With infinitives implying going ( gehen / kommen / fahren / reisen /etc.) to a stated or implied destination: Wir müssen in 10 Minuten nach Hause. We have to [go/drive] home in 10 minutes. Willst du im Sommer nach Deutschland? Do you want to [go/travel] to Germany in the summer? Ich kann jetzt nicht mit dir zu Starbucks. I can’t [go] with you to Starbucks now. In contexts like this where a prefix would be connected to the going verb, such as mit (along) or zurück (back), German retains the prefix but omits the verb: Willst du heute Abend mit ? Do you want to [go/come] along tonight? Er darf nicht zurück . He’s not allowed to [go/come] back. Infinitives implying doing ( machen / tun ): Kannst du das? — Natürlich kann ich das! Can you [do] that? — Of course I can [do] that! It’s not incorrect to include the infinitives in sentences like these — Willst du heute Abend mitkommen ? Er darf nicht zurückgehen . Kannst du das machen ? Natürlich kann ich das machen ! — but it’s colloquially appropriate to omit them if their meaning is clear from the context. H.2
