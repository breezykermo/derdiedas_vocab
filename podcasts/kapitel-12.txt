How the corrections work (the core of the episode):
- The LEARNER makes roughly 12-20 mistakes over the episode. Most should target the

  meinst: ...") or stops briefly to explain the rule in one or two sentences, then gives
  a correct model sentence.
- The LEARNER then repeats the corrected sentence and, where it fits, makes a new
  sentence using the same rule correctly, so the listener hears the right form at least
  twice for every time they hear the wrong one.

- Corrections are kind and encouraging; the LEARNER sometimes self-corrects or
  asks "Warum?" to prompt an explanation.

so the listener should hear as much German as possible.

--- Thema 12.1: Die Grenzen Europas ---

## A. Review: werden
A. Review: werden The verb werden is one of the most frequently used words in German – #9 in the frequency list, in fact – and plays a key role in a number of grammatical structures that will be introduced in this and the following two chapters. Before you begin working on these, it’s important to be familiar with the various tenses and forms of werden . A.1 Werden: Present tense You already know that the basic meaning of werden — used with predicate adjectives and nouns — is to become, to grow or to get, as in becoming / growing / getting old . The present-tense conjugation for du and er/sie/es is irregular: singular plural first-person ich werde wir werden second-person du wirst Sie werden ihr werdet Sie werden third-person er/der Mann wird sie/die Frau wird es/das Kind wird sie/die Leute werden Werden retains these basic meanings regardless of whether it functions as the main verb ( V 1 ) or as a modal infinitive ( V 2 ). V 1 : Sie wird Ärztin, oder? She’s becoming a doctor, isn’t she? V 2 : Nein, mir hat sie gesagt, sie will Musikerin werden . No, she told me she wants to become a musician. V 1 : Du siehst ganz blass aus. Wirst du krank? You look really pale. Are you getting sick? V 2 : Ich darf doch nicht krank werden . Ich hab’ zu viel Arbeit! I just can’t get sick. I’ve got too much work! When werden is followed by a prepositional phrase beginning with zu , its meaning changes slightly to emphasize the process of transformation: to turn into. Das wird zu einem Sicherheitsrisiko. That is becoming (i.e., turning into) a security risk. Leider wird das in einigen Regionen zu einer echten Krise. Unfortunately, that is becoming (i.e., turning into) a genuine crisis in several regions. A.1
A.2 Werden: Past tenses The simple past ( wurde ) and perfect tense ( ist … geworden ) of werden both mean became/was becoming/did become , referring to a process of becoming that took place in the past. Wann wurde Olaf Scholz Bundeskanzler? When did Olaf Scholz become the Federal Chancellor? Wann ist Olaf Scholz Bundeskanzler geworden ? But they are not always synonymous: ist … geworden can also express the idea of has become , emphasizing that the becoming that took place in the past has relevance for the present — a meaning that wurde does not convey. Die Euro-Zone ist größer geworden . The Euro zone has become larger. (implied: its expanded size is current ) Aber die Debatte zum Beitritt der Türkei ist sehr strittig geworden. But the debate concerning Turkey’s admission has become very contentious. (implied: and it still is ) A review of the past-tense forms: 1. Simple past tense The simple past tense of werden is irregular, showing characteristics of both strong and weak verbs. Like all strong verbs, its simple past stem ( wurd- ) involves a vowel change; but unlike strong verbs, it adds endings to that stem in the first- and third-person — endings that differ from weak verb past-tense endings. singular plural first-person ich wurde wir wurden second-person du wurdest Sie wurden ihr wurdet Sie wurden third-person er/der Mann wurde sie/die Frau wurde es/das Kind wurde sie/die Leute wurden 2. Perfect tense The perfect tense of werden is formed by using sein as the auxiliary ( V 1 ), and the past participle geworden ( V 2 ): Wir sind gute Freunde geworden. We became good friends. / We have become good friends. singular plural first-person ich bin … geworden wir sind … geworden second-person du bist … geworden Sie sind … geworden ihr seid … geworden Sie sind … geworden third-person er/der Mann ist … geworden sie/die Frau ist … geworden es/das Kind ist … geworden sie/die Leute sind … geworden 3. Past perfect tense The past perfect tense of werden uses a form of war as the auxiliary ( V 1 ), in tandem with geworden ( V 2 ): Wir waren schon gute Freunde geworden. We had already become good friends. (Granted, you won’t see this particular tense often, and it’s unlikely you’ll need to produce it very frequently yourself, but we include it here to cover all the possible options.) singular plural first-person ich war … geworden wir waren … geworden second-person du warst … geworden Sie waren … geworden ihr wart … geworden Sie waren … geworden third-person er/der Mann war … geworden sie/die Frau war … geworden es/das Kind war … geworden sie/die Leute waren … geworden A.2

## B. Future tense
B. Future tense B.1 German forms the future tense by combining werden ( V 1 ) in the present tense with an infinitive ( V 2 ), following the same pattern used for modal verbs. Wir werden dir helfen . We will/are going to help you. Was wirst du jetzt machen ? What will you/are you going to do now? Ich werde nie verstehen , warum … I will never/am never going to understand why … Notice that this construction translates into English as both going to and will . But be careful with the will option: I will and Ich will are not synonymous. Ich will means I want to in the present tense, whereas I will makes reference to the future, and is translated with ich werde …. In dependent clauses, the conjugated form of werden moves to the very end of the clause, i.e., after the infinitive: Ich verspreche, dass wir dir helfen werden . I promise that we’re going to/we’ll help you. Weißt du, was du jetzt machen wirst ? Do you know what you will/are going to do now? Manchmal frage ich mich, ob ich je verstehen werde, warum … Sometimes I wonder if I will ever understand why … B.1
B.2. German speakers use the future tense far more sparingly than English speakers do. As long as a future context is made clear — by means of adverbial modifiers such as später / nächstes Jahr / in zwei Wochen / dann or similar elements — Germans tend to use the present tense. While it wouldn’t be grammatically wrong to use the future tense when a future reference is clear, to a German speaker it can sound overemphasized, a little like an English speaker who says I shall do that . Keep in mind that English also uses the present tense to express future meaning, as in We are driving home tomorrow , but this normally requires the use of an auxiliary verb, as in this example: are driving . In German, the preferred way of expressing this sentence would be Wir fahren morgen nach Hause . Compare: Wir machen das später. We’ll do that later. Wann verstehst du das endlich? When are you finally going to understand that? In situations where this use of the present tense could lead to misunderstanding, German uses the future tense to clarify the intended meaning: Sie wird bestimmt da sein . She'll definitely be there. [compare: Sie ist bestimmt da. She's definitely there. ] The future tense is also used to add emphasis: Ich werde dich nie verlassen! I’m never going to leave you! / I will never leave you! And it’s used to refer speculatively to a distant point in the future, even if the future context is clear: Wie wird Europa in 100 Jahren aussehen ? What will Europe look like in 100 years? B.2
B.3. To express modal verbs in the future tense ( You will have to … / We will be able to … ), German uses werden as V 1 and places the modal verb (in its infinitive form) at the very end of the clause. Du wirst dich darauf verlassen müssen . You’ll have to depend on it. Wir werden um 2 Uhr essen können . We’ll be able to eat at 2 o’clock. Er wird es ändern wollen . He will want to change it. Germans use this tense not so much to distinguish between having to do something now as opposed to having to something later , but rather to express the idea that the modality in play (must/can/wants to) is most likely to be the case, as opposed to definitely being the case. In the first example above, the point is that it's most likely you'll have to depend on it , rather than you'll definitely have to depend on it. In the second example, it may not be the case that we'll eat at two o'clock, but most likely we will. And the third example suggests that he'll most likely want to change it — as opposed to implying that he definitely wants to change it . B.3

## D. Nouns and pronouns: Word order
D. Nouns and pronouns: Word order D.1. When noun/pronoun accusative and dative objects appear together, they usually do so in conjunction with verbs that call for a three-way interaction: Someone (nominative) does something (accusative) to or for someone (dative). The core vocabulary contains a number of verbs that make use of this interaction: bringen to bring empfehlen to recommend erklären to explain erzählen to tell geben to give holen to fetch, get kaufen to buy öffnen to open sagen to say zeigen to show D.1
D.2 The sentences in Übung 12.12 are intended to make you pause and ask yourself: If a pronoun and a noun appear together, which comes first? What about two nouns? Does it matter? In most cases, this isn’t a problem for English speakers, since English and German overlap to a great extent in how they handle this. But not completely: English has two ways of expressing a dative object ( I gave the man the money / I gave the money to the man ). And English varies regarding which of these formats can be used with which verbs. With give , for example, you can use both, as the examples above indicate. But if you try doing that with explain (* I explained the policeman …) or say ( * I said them … ), you see the problem. German, by contrast, is consistent on both counts: It normally uses the dative case (rather than the to/for construction) to indicate indirect objects. It this format consistently for all the verbs listed in D.1. The commentary that follows explains in more detail how this works when dative and accusative objects are expressed by two nouns; by a noun/pronoun combination; and by two pronouns. D.2
D.3 Noun + noun In sentences where dative object nouns and accusative object nouns appear side by side, the dative noun comes first . English works this way as well: I told the children a story. He bought his brother a Swiss watch. But English can also use the to/for construction with some of these: I told a story to the children. He bought a Swiss watch for his brother. German, however, prefers the “dative object first” format: Ich erzählte den Kindern eine Geschichte. Er kaufte seinem Bruder eine Schweizer Uhr. More examples, using verbs from the list in D.1: bringen Sie brachte ihren Mitbewohnern eine Pizza. She brought her roommates a pizza. / She brought a pizza for her roommates. empfehlen Sie haben den Touristen ein Restaurant empfohlen. They recommended a restaurant to the tourists. erklären Sie hat den Studenten die Situation erklärt. She explained the situation to the students. sagen Du musst deiner Freundin die Wahrheit sagen. You have to tell your girlfriend the truth. / You have to tell the truth to your girlfriend. zeigen Der Schiri zeigte dem Spieler die rote Karte. The ref showed the player the red card. / The ref showed the red card to the player. D.3
D.4 Pronoun + noun The sentences in Übung 12.13 illustrate an important rule: When dative objects and accusative objects are a combination of nouns and pronouns that stand side by side, the pronoun always comes first — regardless of its case. bringen Sie brachte es ihrer Familie. She brought it to her family. empfehlen Sie haben ihnen ein Restaurant empfohlen. They recommended a restaurant for them. erklären Wir erklärten es dem Polizisten. We explained it to the policeman. erzählen Er erzählte uns eine Story. He told us a story. geben Ich gebe dir fünf Punkte. I’ll give you five points. holen Kannst du uns etwas Essen holen? Can you fetch/get us some food? kaufen Sie hat es ihrem Vater gekauft. She bought it for her father. öffnen Soll ich Ihnen die Tür öffnen? Should I open the door for you? sagen Du musst es deinen Freunden sagen. You have to say it to your friends. zeigen Er hat ihm die rote Karte gezeigt. He showed him the red card. D.4
D.5 Pronoun + pronoun If both the accusative and the dative object have already been previously mentioned, so that a subsequent comment referring to them contains two pronouns, the accusative pronoun comes first . (Notice that English does the same thing, and uses the to/for construction to express the indirect object). bringen Sie brachte es ihnen . She brought it to them. empfehlen Sie haben es ihnen empfohlen. They recommended it to them. erklären Wir erklärten es ihm . We explained it to him. erzählen Er erzählte sie uns . He told it to us. geben Ich gebe sie dir . I’ll give them to you. holen Kannst du es uns holen? Can you fetch it / get it for us? kaufen Sie hat es ihm gekauft. She bought it for him. öffnen Soll ich sie Ihnen öffnen? Should I open it for you? sagen Du musst es ihnen sagen. You have to say it to them. zeigen Er hat sie ihm gezeigt. He showed it to him. To sum up: In sentences where accusative and dative objects appear side by side after V 1 : Two nouns: The dative noun comes first. Noun/pronoun combination: The pronoun comes first. Two pronouns: The accusative pronoun comes first. D.5

E.3 Fixed combination: da-/wo-compounds If the object of the preposition in a fixed combination ( denken + an / Reaktion + auf ) is a pronoun, German uses a da- compound for statements and a wo- compound for questions. (Exception: When the pronoun refers to a person, a gendered pronoun must be used.) Some examples: Die Semesterpause? Ja, ich denke oft daran. The semester break? Yes, I think about it often. Hast du die Debatte gehört? Die Reaktion darauf in den Medien hat mich überrascht. Did you hear the debate? The reaction to it in the media surprised me. Woran denkst du gerade? What are you thinking about right now? Wovor hast du Angst ? What are you afraid/scared of? Worüber willst du schreiben ? What do you want to write about? Remember: da- and wo- can only refer to things, concepts, or abstractions — not to people: I’m thinking about it = Ich denke daran . BUT: I’m thinking about her/him. = Ich denke an sie/ihn. An -r- is inserted between the two elements if the preposition begins with a vowel: da r um / da r aus / da r über / wo r an / wo r über / etc. da- compounds can be formed with most accusative and dative prepositions, but not with außer , ohne , and seit , and not with the genitive prepositions. wo- compounds can also be formed with most accusative and dative prepositions, but not with außer , hinter , ohne , seit , and zwischen , and not with the genitive prepositions. ## F. Correlative conjunctions
F. Correlative conjunctions German also has a number of fixed combinations using conjunctions, often called correlative conjunctions . The most commonly used example is entweder … oder. They are considered correlative because they consist of related words and always appear together in a fixed sequence. Just as either (used as a conjunction) is always followed up with or , entweder always appear with oder , regardless of whether they connect words, phrases or entire clauses: Er heißt entweder Timo oder Timm, glaube ich. Wir können entweder bei McDonalds oder bei Nordsee essen. Entweder kommst du mit, oder du bleibst den ganzen Abend zu Hause. Two additional correlative conjunctions are included in the core vocabulary of this chapter and are discussed here and in F.2. F.1 weder ... noch Weder … noch (neither ... nor) functions exactly like its English equivalent, with weder prefacing the first element (which can be a word, phrase or an entire clause) and noch prefacing the second. Er kann weder singen noch tanzen, aber er meint, er kann beides. He can neither sing nor dance, but he thinks he can do both. Für meinen Aufsatz habe ich weder genug Ideen noch genug Zeit. For my essay, I have neither enough ideas nor enough time. F.1
F.2 sowohl ... als auch Imagine that you’ve visited several cities in Switzerland and you want to mention this to someone. You could simply list the cities, using and as a connecting conjunction: I visited Berne and Zurich. But if you wanted to emphasize that it was two cities, not just one, you might use both to highlight this fact: I visited both Bern and Zurich. Or you could replace and with a different conjunction: I visited Bern as well as Zurich. German uses sowohl … als auch to convey this added emphasis, by prefacing the first element with sowohl and the second element with als auch: Ich habe sowohl Bern als auch Zürich besucht. A few more examples: Fast alle Züricher sprechen sowohl Französisch als auch Deutsch. Almost everyone in Zurich speaks both French and German. / … French as well as German. Die Schweiz ist sowohl für die Schönheit der Natur als auch ihren Reichtum bekannt. Switzerland is known both for its natural beauty and its wealth. /… for its natural beauty as well as its wealth. NB: English speakers can fall into two traps with this construction. If they’re thinking (in English) of both ... and ..., they may be tempted to use beide ... und ..., which doesn’t work in German. On the other hand, the English construction as well as only appears before the second element, so that English speakers may forget to use sowohl before the first element. Remember: Both sowohl and als auch are required. [Pun intended.] F.2

## (G.1)
G.1 Present participles German has two kinds of participles: Past participles the V 2 elements in the Perfect tense: Sie hat mindestens drei Sprachen gesprochen . Wir haben das Zimmer renoviert . adjectival modifiers, indicating that something has been done: ein gesprochenes Wort das renovierte Zimmer Present participles adjectival modifiers indicating ongoing action, like these in English: a singing waiter the missing information emerging markets The formation of present participles in German is straightforward: Where English adds -ing to the root form of the verb, German adds -d- to the infinitive, followed by the contextually appropriate adjective ending: singen + - d- → singend- : ein singen d er Kellner° fehlen + - d- → fehlend- : die fehlen d e Information entstehen + - d- → entstehend- : entstehen d e Märkte Some additional examples: das spielende Kind the playing child schon existierende Verhältnisse already existing relationships in einer einladenden Atmosphäre in an inviting atmosphere ein prägender Moment a formative (lit.: “forming”) moment eine entsprechende Aufgabe a corresponding assignment G.1

H.3 Dative reflexive pronouns The dative reflexive pronouns are as follows: singular plural nominative dative nominative dative ich mir wir uns du Sie dir sich ihr Sie euch sich er sie es sich sich sich sie sich These can serve as: indirect objects Mario holt sich etwas zum Mittagessen. Mario is getting himself something for lunch. objects of dative verbs (helfen / schaden / antworten / etc.) In solchen Situationen kann ich mir einfach nicht helfen. In such situations I just can’t help myself. objects of dative prepositions Wir haben ein paar Leute zu uns eingeladen. We invited a few people over to our place. objects of variable prepositions (when these require the dative case) Solche Leute haben nur Interesse an sich selber. People like that only have an interest in themselves. H.3
H.4 Selbst / selber Selbst (introduced in Chapter 4) and selber are used in tandem with reflexive pronouns for emphasis, much like English _____self . They often appear along with reflexive pronouns in contexts that normally call for a non-reflexive object. Wir haben uns selbst/selber überrascht. We surprised ourselves. Im Film stellt der Schauspieler sich selbst/selber dar. In the film, the actor portrays himself. Selbst and selber can also be used by themselves as adverbs to emphasize the associated pronoun or noun. Ich will das selbst/selber herausfinden. I want to find that out for myself. Like sogar, selbst (but not selber ) can preface another element to create the same emphasis conveyed in English with even ______, usually in the front field of an independent clause. Selbst/sogar du sollst das wissen! Even you are supposed to know that! Selbst/Sogar am Wochenende arbeitet sie 12 Stunden pro Tag. Even on the weekend she works 12 hours a day. H.4
I.1 Review: Reflexive verbs As the charts and examples in H.2 and H.3 above remind you, reflexive pronouns can take on multiple functions: direct objects, indirect objects and objects of prepositions. But most often they are associated with »reflexive« verbs — verbs that require a reflexive object pronoun in order to convey particular meanings, often in contexts where English does not use a reflexive pronoun. In Chapter 7 you saw how reflexive verbs can be grouped into three categories, shown here with all the verbs in each category from the core vocabulary so far. [NB: You've already learned most of these verbs; they are presented here and in the next two sections in order to remind you how they function.] Verbs that take an accusative reflexive pronoun: sich entscheiden to decide among various options sich fühlen to feel/be [tired, energized, etc.] sich setzen to be seated, sit [down] sich verhalten to behave, act Warum hast du dich für Princeton entschieden? Why did you decide on Princeton? Er verhält sich heute ganz komisch. He’s behaving very oddly today. Bitte setzen Sie sich ! Please sit down / be seated! I.1
I.2 Verbs that take a dative reflexive pronoun as well as an accusative direct object: sich etwas leisten to afford something sich etwas überlegen to ponder/consider something sich etwas vor·stellen to imagine something/picture something in one’s imagination sich etwas wünschen to wish for something In the following examples, the dative reflexive is highlighted in bold, and the accusative direct object is underlined: Du musst dir sein Angebot gut überlegen. You’ll have to consider his offer carefully. Zum Geburtstag wünsche ich mir einen großen Schokoladenkuchen . For my birthday, I’m wishing for a big chocolate cake. Wie kannst du dir so was leisten? How can you afford something like that? I.2
I.3 Verbs that take an accusative reflexive pronoun and a prepositional phrase: sich beschäftigen + mit to occupy oneself with/devote oneself to Sie beschäftigt sich mit alter Kunst. sich ein·stellen + auf [Acc.] to adapt to, become accustomed to Man muss sich auf das Wetter hier einstellen. sich erinnern + an [Acc.] to remember Erinnerst du dich an mich? sich freuen + auf [Acc.] to look forward to Ich freue mich sehr auf das Wochenende. + über [Acc.] to be happy about Freut ihr euch über das Ergebnis vom Test? sich interessieren + für to be interested in Wer interessiert sich für ein Kartenspiel? sich kümmern + um to take care of, deal with Ich kümmere mich um das Essen. sich verlassen + auf [Acc.] to depend on Du kannst dich immer auf mich verlassen!
