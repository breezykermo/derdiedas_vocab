## A. der/die/das
A. der/die/das So: Why is this program called der | die | das ? Go back to the beginning of Interaktion 1.5 and find the following nouns. What words do you see highlighted in front of them? ____ Mann ____ Königin ____ Männlein Each of these nouns — and every other noun in German — has a grammatical gender indicated by the definite article (the word for the ) associated with it: der for masculine die for feminine das for neuter. And these three words, along with their variations for plural forms and various cases, are the most frequently used words in German. (You’ve probably noticed another important feature of nouns in German: they’re always capitalized, regardless of where they appear in a sentence.) Students often ask: What are the rules for determining if a noun is masculine, feminine or neuter — besides cases of natural gender, like der Mann and die Frau ? Answer: While there are some features that dictate grammatical gender ( das Männlein because of the suffix –lein, for example), there are so many rules (and exceptions to the rules) that it’s ultimately more efficient simply to learn each noun with its grammatical gender as you encounter it. Say the article and the noun out loud together; write them together; use them together as often as possible, until you think of them as a unit. A

## B. Subjects + Verbs: ich / du / Sie
B. Subjects + Verbs: ich / du / Sie Frau Schmitt, haben Sie viel° Arbeit? — Ja, ich habe viel Arbeit ... ich I du you [familiar; singular] Sie you [formal; singular] B.1 Another basic feature of German is the way verbs change in order to correspond with the sentence subject. This is especially true of the two most frequently used verbs in German, sein (to be) and haben (to have) . Here are the forms you use when referring to yourself (first-person singular) or speaking to another person (second-person singular): sein ich bin I am du bist you (familiar) are Sie sind you (formal) are haben ich habe I have du hast you (familiar) have Sie haben you (formal) have B.1
B.2 Most other Germans verbs conjugate by using the verb stem — the part of the verb that remains when the final -en is removed — and adding endings that correspond to the subject of the verb. Here are the stems for some of the verbs you’ve encountered so far: arbeiten → arbeit– finden → find– fragen → frag– heißen → heiß– hören → hör– sagen → sag– spielen → spiel– studieren → studier– B.2
B.3 This is how most verbs conjugate in the present tense for ich / du / Sie : spielen: spiel- ich -e → ich spiel e … du -st → du spiel st … Sie -en → Sie spiel en … A few things to keep in mind when conjugating verbs with du : If the verb stem ends with -t or -d, you add an extra -e- before the -st , making it easier to pronounce: arbeiten: arbeit- du -(e)st → du arbeit est … If the verb stem ends with -s / -ß / -z , you drop the -s from the -st ending: heißen: heiß - du -t → du heiß t … B.3

## D. Plural nouns
D. Plural nouns In German, plural nouns can be recognized by the article used with them and (in most cases) by a change in the noun itself, similar to English. First, the article: All gender distinctions disappear when nouns are in their plural form, so that they all use the definite article die: das Auto (sing.) → die Autos (pl.). Like English, and unlike some other European languages, there is no indefinite plural article in German. In most situations, the absence of an article with a plural noun in English (e.g., I have problems ) is mirrored in German when expressing an indefinite plural noun: Ich habe Probleme . Noun plurals are formed in German by applying one of the following procedures to the singular form, depending on the noun. Here are the categories, using words from the lists in section C above: what happens to the singular noun: how the noun is listed in a dictionary: how the plural noun actually appears: no change der Computer, - das Semester, - die Computer die Semester add umlaut to the stem vowel der Vater, ¨- die Mutter, ¨- die Väter die Mütter add -e or -er der Film, -e das Bild, -er die Filme die Bilder add umlaut, then -e or -er die Stadt, ¨-e das Land, ¨-er die Städte die Länder add -n or -(n)en die Lampe, -n der Student, -en die Studentin, -nen die Lampen die Studenten die Studentinnen add -s die Party, -s das Auto, -s die Partys die Autos D

## F. Pronouns and gender
F. Pronouns and gender Every noun in German is assigned a grammatical gender ( der | die | das ), and these gender distinctions apply as well to the corresponding pronouns ( er | sie | es ). F.1 English speakers intuitively make the connection between natural gender and these pronouns: Der Student? → Er ist … The (male) student? He is … Die Professorin? → Sie ist … The (female) professor? She is … But German makes this connection for inanimate objects as well, using er | sie | es to stand in for masculine, feminine and neuter nouns, where English simply uses it: Der Film? → Er ist … The film? It is … Die Aufgabe? → Sie ist … The assignment? It is … Das Buch? → Es ist … The book? It is … Since plural nouns show no gender distinctions, there is only one pronoun for they: sie die Studenten the students die Städte the cities die Filme the films die Bücher the books → und sie sind … and they are … There is considerable debate in Germany, Austria and Switzerland surrounding the use of gendered pronouns and masculine plural forms meant to refer to everyone (e.g., Studenten in the list above). If you're interested, you can find a short introductory NPR article here — and your instructor can point you to more resources. F.1
F.2 Some languages (such as Spanish) delete subject pronouns once the subject has been established, rather than repeating it in subsequent clauses or sentences. But in most cases, German requires a subject — either a pronoun or a noun — in every clause or sentence. Der Mann heißt Alexander. The man’s name is Alexander. Er ist 30 Jahre alt. He’s 30 years old. Er wohnt in Berlin. He lives in Berlin. Er spielt Fußball jeden Sonntag. He plays soccer every Sunday. That said, it’s certainly possible for a subject to be connected with more than one verb in an extended clause: Er ist 30 Jahre alt, wohnt in Berlin und spielt Fußball. But if the information is divided into separate clauses or sentences, each usually has a noun or pronoun subject. F.2

## G. Subjects + Verbs: wir / ihr / Sie
G. Subjects + Verbs: wir / ihr / Sie wir we ihr you (all) [familiar] Sie you (all) [formal] G.1 Below are the verb forms of sein and haben that you use when talking about a group that includes you ( wir ); when addressing a group of people with whom you use the familiar form for you ( ihr ); and when addressing a group of people with whom you use the formal form for you ( Sie ): sein haben wir we sind haben ihr you (all) [familiar] seid habt Sie you (all) [formal] sind haben NB: Sie is the formal form for you when addressing either one person or several people, and the verb endings are identical for both situations. This means that only the context — not the grammatical form — can tell you if Sie is plural or singular in a given sentence. G.1
G.2. For most other verbs, the forms used with wir, ihr and Sie are highly predictable — just the stem plus the appropriate ending. (Notice that the endings are identical for wir and Sie .) spiel- find- wir [stem] + en spielen finden ihr [stem] + t spielt findet Sie [stem] + en spielen finden As you’ve learned for other forms, when the verb stem ends with -d or -t , an -e- is inserted before the -t : Wie find e t ihr das Essen hier? What do you (all) think of the food here? Ihr arbeit e t zu viel! You (all) work too much! G.2

## H. I like ... / like to ...
H. I like ... / like to ... To express an opinion, it's helpful to know how to say I like that or I like doing that. Here are several options for doing so; you’ll learn more in the chapters to come. H.1 To say you like (or don’t like ) something or someone, as represented by a noun or a pronoun, you can use these expressions: Ich finde [X] + [Adjektiv]. Ich finde den Film »Dumm und Dümmer« fantastisch! Spinnst du? ° Ich finde »Dumm und Dümmer« nur dumm. [X] gefällt mir. [literally: ... is pleasing to me .] Der Film gefällt mir. [X] gefällt mir nicht. [literally: ... is not pleasing to me .] Was? Also, er gefällt mir gar nicht. If [X] is plural, the verb takes the plural form gefallen : Meine Kurse gefallen mir! H.1
H.2 To express the idea of liking to do something , e.g., I like to play the piano, German uses the adverb gern [literally: gladly ] after the verb. You’ll learn more about this later, but you can form simple sentences with it now: arbeiten gern schreiben gern sein gern gehen gern Ich arbeite gern . Schreibst du gern ? Er ist gern zu Hause. Wir gehen gern in die Stadt. I like to work. Do you like to write? He likes to be at home. We like to go into the city. To say you don’t like doing something, use nicht gern. (You can intensify the phrase by adding gar : Ich mache diese Aufgaben gar nicht gern !) arbeiten nicht gern schreiben nicht gern sein nicht gern gehen nicht gern Ich arbeite gar nicht gern . Schreibst du nicht gern ? Er ist nicht gern zu Hause. Wir gehen nicht gern in die Stadt. I really don’t like to work. Don’t you like to write? He doesn’t like to be at home. We don’t like to go into the city. H.2

## I. Time/calendar expressions
I. Time/calendar expressions I.1 To say that something is happening on Monday or in the afternoon , you use the contracted prepositional phrase am ( an + dem on the ) along with the day of the week or the part of the day. (Exception: in der Nacht at night ) am Montag Dienstag Mittwoch Donnerstag Freitag Samstag [ southern and western Germany, Austria, and Switzerland ] Sonnabend [ northern Germany ] Sonntag am Morgen° Nachmittag° Abend in der Nacht° Am Dienstag haben wir ein Quiz in Mathe. On Tuesday we’re having a quiz in math. Und dann am Nachmittag spiele ich Hockey. And then in the afternoon I’m playing hockey. Depending on the context, these references can be general or specific. Like English, German allows you to add an –s to any of these elements, including Nacht , for general rather than specific time references, as in Mondays I like to… Montags habe ich einen Kurs um 10 Uhr. Mondays / On Mondays I have a course at 10:00 AM. Ich gehe freitags gern mit Freunden ins Café. Fridays / On Fridays I like to go with friends to a café. Morgens habe ich Kurse, nachmittags schlafe ich, und nachts arbeite ich. In the morning I have courses, in the afternoon I sleep, and at night I work. I.1
I.2 To refer to specific parts of a day, the day and time segment are joined to form one word: Montag : Montag morgen Montag nachmittag Montag abend Am is also used with these compounds: Am Freitagabend spielt das Orchester. On Friday evening, the orchestra is playing. Sie kommt am Montagmorgen . She’s coming on Monday morning. Er arbeitet nicht am Sonntagnachmittag . He doesn’t work on Sunday afternoon. With heute , however, the two elements remain separated: Heute Morgen habe ich eine Prüfung. This morning (lit.: today in the morning) I have a test. Heute Nachmittag arbeite ich im Labor. This afternoon (lit.: today in the afternoon) I'm working in the laboratory. Aber heute Abend gehe ich in ein Konzert. But tonight (lit.: today in the evening) I’m going to a concert. NB: Nacht isn’t quite the same as night , which in English can refer either to the time between dinner and bedtime (as in Friday Night Lights ), or to the time between going to bed and getting up. German uses Abend for the former and Nacht for the latter. I.2

## J. Subjects + Verbs: Summary
J. Subjects + Verbs: Summary In this chapter you've worked with three kinds of sentence subjects: first-person : the speaker, either alone (I) or as part of a group (we) second-person : addressing one person ( you: familiar and formal), or a group of people ( you all: familiar and formal) third-person : referring to someone or something else, singular or plural, represented by a noun (the weekend; my friends) or a pronoun (it; they) J.1 These subjects correlate with endings that are added to the verb stem: machen → mach- singular plural first-person ich mach e wir mach en second-person du mach st [familiar] Sie mach en [formal] ihr mach t [familiar] Sie mach en [formal] third-person er/der Mann mach t sie/die Frau mach t es/das Kind mach t sie/die Leute mach en When a verb stem ends in –t or –d , the -t and -st endings become -et and -est : arbeiten → arbeit- / finden → find- singular plural first-person ich arbeite wir arbeiten second-person du arbeit est [familiar] Sie arbeiten [formal] ihr arbeit et [familiar] Sie arbeiten [formal] third-person er/der Mann arbeit et sie/die Frau arbeit et es/das Kind arbeit et sie/die Leute arbeiten J.1
J.2 For sein and haben — the two most commonly used verbs in German — the present-tense forms are irregular: sein singular plural first-person ich bin wir sind second-person du bist [familiar] Sie sind [formal] ihr seid [familiar] Sie sind [formal] third-person er/der Computer ist sie/die Tür ist es/das Handy ist sie/die Leute sind haben singular plural first-person ich habe wir haben second-person du hast [familiar] Sie haben [formal] ihr habt [familiar] Sie haben [formal] third-person er/der Computer hat sie/die Tür hat es/das Handy hat sie/die Leute haben J.2
