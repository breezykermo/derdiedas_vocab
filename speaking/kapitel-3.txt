--- Thema 3.1: Ein ganz normaler Sonntag ---

## A. Saying some: etwas / einige / ein paar
A. Saying some: etwas / einige / ein paar A.1 Etwas You’ve already learned the basic meaning of etwas ( something ), when it is used like a noun or pronoun: Ich lese etwas . I’m reading something. Später essen wir etwas . Later we’ll eat something. Etwas stinkt! Something stinks! When speaking or writing casually, German speakers frequently shorten etwas to was, as long as it’s not the first element in the sentence: Ich lese was für meinen Philosophie-Kurs. Später essen wir was zusammen. But etwas can take on additional meanings, depending on the context: Etwas can be paired with an adjective, as English does in phrases such as something important and something new . In German, the adjective is capitalized and takes the ending -es . Das ist etwas Toll es . That’s something terrific. Ich habe etwas Wichtig es zu sagen. I have something important to say. Here too, people often use was in casual speech and writing rather than etwas , unless it’s the first element in a sentence: Wir machen was Schönes heute. We’re doing something nice today. Hast du was Wichtiges zu sagen? Do you have something important to say? To express the opposite — nothing important — you use nichts (with an added -s ). Er hat nichts Wichtiges zu sagen. He has nothing important to say. / He doesn’t have anything important to say. Etwas can also modify an adjective or a singular noun, meaning somewhat / a little / some (sing.) . Ich stehe etwas später auf. I’ll get up a little later. Der Film ist etwas lang, oder? The film is a little/somewhat long, isn’t it? Hast du etwas Zeit? Do you have a little/some time? A.1
A.2. Ein paar / einige Etwas can only be used to modify singular nouns. To express the idea of some with a plural noun, you must use ein paar (a few/a couple of) or einige (a few/several): Einige Freunde von mir … A few/some friends of mine … Sie hat ein paar neue Fotos auf Instagram. She has a couple of new pictures on Instagram. In the same way, viel (much/a lot of) is used only with singular nouns, and viele (many/a lot of) only with plural nouns. For English speakers, learning to make these distinctions takes some practice, since some and a lot of can be used in English with both singular and plural nouns. It may help to think of viel simply as much , and viele as many. That way you can avoid saying things that sound like * much friends or * many food . A.2

## B. Stem-changing verbs
B. Stem-changing verbs While working through the Interaktionen in this section, you probably noticed that several verbs showed a change in the vowel of the verb stem in certain conjugations: Ich nehme … Der Vater nimmt … Die Mutter und die Töchter halten … Der Vater hält … B.1 A relatively small number of German verbs require a stem-vowel change when they conjugate with du and er/sie/es . (It’s not an exact analogy, but think of the difference in pronunciation between I say vs. he/she says , or between I do vs. he/she does .) There are four vowel changes possible, depending on the verb: e → ie lesen → lies- Liest du gern eBooks? Ich nicht. sehen → sieh- Er sieht das Problem. e → i sprechen → sprich- Mein Professor spricht zu schnell! essen → iss- Marco isst heute mit mir. a → ä schlafen → schläf- Du schläfst nicht genug. au → äu laufen → läuf- Die Frau läuft schnell. With all other subjects [ich / Sie / wir / ihr / sie (pl.)], these verbs conjugate regularly, using the stem derived from the infinitive. Lesen Sie gern eBooks? Ich sehe das Problem. Sie sprechen zu schnell! Meine Eltern essen heute mit mir. Warum schlafen so viele Studenten in ihren Kursen? Ich laufe gern, aber nicht besonders schnell. From now on, verbs like this will be indicated in the vocabulary lists with the er/sie/es form shown before the two past-tense forms (introduced in chapters to follow): lesen [ liest | las | gelesen] to read laufen [ läuft | lief | ist gelaufen] to run B.1
B.2 The verbs halten and nehmen (as well as einladen to invite, which appears later in this chapter) have additional irregularities when they conjugate with du and er/sie/es : halten ( hält/hältst ) → No ending is added to the stem for the er/sie/es form; and no -e- is inserted between the stem and the -st ending. nehmen ( nimmt/nimmst ) → There’s a change in the consonants as well as the vowel in the stem. einladen ( einlädt/einlädst ) → No -e- is inserted between the stem and the -t/-st endings. And remember that sein, haben and werden have irregular forms as well: sein (to be) Ich bin gestresst. Du bist auch gestresst. Aber das Kind ist nicht gestresst. haben (to have) Ich habe viel zu tun. Du hast auch viel zu tun. Der Mann hat nicht so viel zu tun. werden (to become) Ich werde Mechaniker. Du wirst Astronautin, oder? Die Frau wird Professorin. B.2

--- Thema 3.2: Sonntags geht man ... ---

## C. Ich gehe [joggen]
C. Ich gehe [joggen] The use of go in phrases such as I’m going shopping or She often goes jogging has a close parallel in German: Ich gehe joggen . I’m going jogging. Wir gehen laufen . We're going running. Sie geht schwimmen . She’s going swimming. Gehst du einkaufen/shoppen ? Are you going shopping? As the examples show, gehen conjugates with the subject, while the English –ing verb remains an infinitive in German. There is one important difference, however. In English, additional information can be inserted at various points — but never between go and _____ing: She often goes jogging . She goes jogging every morning. Does she ever go jogging with friends? She never goes jogging in the winter. German, however, inserts all such information between gehen and the infinitive — no matter how much information there may be — and in so doing splits gehen and the second verb apart: Sie geht oft joggen . Sie geht jeden Morgen joggen . Geht sie je mit Freunden joggen ? Sie geht im Winter nie joggen . Think about how this appeared in the Interaktionen you just worked with: Ich gehe früh joggen . Er geht die Eltern besuchen . Man geht sonntags nicht einkaufen . Wann geht man in den USA wählen ? Caution: I’m going jogging is not quite the same as I’m going to jog . In the latter sentence, going to indicates the future tense, which German expresses with a different structure (Chapter 12: Thema 1). While it’s true that I’m going jogging is routinely said prior to the activity itself and thus implies future activity, it's best to think of gehen + Infinitiv as I go ing , rather than I'm going to . C

## D. Adjective endings: Nominative
D. Adjective endings: Nominative D.1 Perhaps you've already noticed that adjectives in German show different endings, and that these can vary, as you see in the endings for neu and normal in these two headlines: So: How do you know which ending to use? The rules are straightforward enough that you should be able to deduce them for yourself on the basis of a few examples: Der Computer ist neu . Das ist mein neuer Computer. Hier ist der neue Computer. Die Stadt ist alt . Das ist eine sehr alte Stadt. Diese alte Stadt gefällt mir. Das Wochenende ist schön . Das ist ein schönes Wochenende. Dieses schöne Wochenende ist viel zu kurz. Diese Filme sind alt. Diese alten Filme gefallen mir. Alte Filme gefallen mir. Judging from these sentences, what endings do you use after… an adjective that appears after the noun it modifies (i.e., at the end of a sentence): an adjective that appears between an ein -word and a noun: an adjective that appears between a der -word and a noun: an adjective with no preceding word, before a noun: D.1
D.2 Here are the rules: Adjectives that come after the nouns they modify — following verbs such as sein, bleiben and werden — take no endings: Das Problem ist schwer . Die Musik wird zu laut . Wir bleiben ganz ruhig . Er färbt° seine Haare gern blau . Adjectives that come before the nouns they modify require endings: Ein -words don’t distinguish between masculine and neuter nouns in the nominative case, so the adjective endings take on that function, using -er and -es . Adjectives following the feminine article eine take the ending -e : ein/unser/kein Kind → ein/unser/kein jung es Kind ein/ihr/kein Mann → ein/ihr/kein alt er Mann eine/seine/keine Frau → eine/seine/keine glücklich e Frau Der -words already show the -er / -es distinction between masculine and neuter nouns, so the ending following the adjective is just -e . Adjectives following feminine singular der -words likewise take an -e ending: das/dieses/jedes Kind → das/dieses/jedes jung e Kind der/dieser/jeder Mann → der/dieser/jeder alt e Mann die/diese/jede Frau → die/diese/jede glücklich e Frau If there’s no preceding word — which often happens with plural nouns — the adjective ending approximates the definite article that would be used: Leute → jung e Leute ( think : di e Leute) Musik → gut e Musik ( think : di e Musik) Sport → gut er Sport ( think : d er Sport) Deutsch → gut es Deutsch ( think : d as Deutsch) What about plural nouns that have preceding words? Easy: the ending is always -en : die jung en Kinder diese jung en Kinder keine jung en Kinder alle jung en Kinder If multiple adjectives appear in the same grammatical context, they all take the same ending: ein klein es , energisch es , glücklich es Kind ein hart er aber fair er Sport schön e , alt e , klassisch e Musik kurz e , blau e Haare Got all that? Good. It’s actually easier in practice than you might think. D.2

--- Thema 3.3: Sonntagnachmittag ---

## E. Verbs with separable prefixes
E. Verbs with separable prefixes E.1 German has a number of verbs that are trennbar (separable) , which means that one element of the verb can separate, or split off, from the core verb. You’ve seen several of these in the Interaktionen above: an·fangen: Wir fangen um 3.30 Uhr an . an·rufen: Annika ruft ihren Bruder an . auf·stehen: Ich stehe sonntags um 11 Uhr auf . aus·gehen: Man geht am Nachmittag aus . ein·laden: Ich lade dich zu einem Kaffee ein . zurück·kommen: Wir kommen etwas später zurück . There are analogous forms in English, some of which allow for variations : to turn off: I’m turning the light off . / I'm turning off the light. to call up: She called him up last night. E.1
E.2 In German, the separation of these verb elements follows specific rules: In the infinitive form, the elements don’t split apart. The separable element remains attached to the core verb as a prefix: an fangen / an rufen / auf stehen / aus gehen / zurück kommen . When the prefix splits off, it normally moves to the end of the clause. Sometimes you’ll see short phrases (beginning with zu ) at the very end of the clause, following the separated prefix: Es fängt jetzt an zu regnen . It’s starting to rain now. But usually the prefix moves all the way to the end of the clause: Wir fangen mit dem ersten Kapitel an . We’re starting with the first chapter. So: When does the prefix separate from the core verb? Answer: Whenever the core verb conjugates with the subject of the clause. Otherwise the two elements remain together. (This rules applies to independent clauses, which are the only clauses you’ve worked with so far, so for now it’s all you need to remember. We’ll look at other kinds of clauses starting in Chapter 10.) When separable-prefix verbs appear in lists or dictionary entries, a dot or vertical line is usually inserted between the prefix and the core verb to indicate that the prefix can separate: an·fangen an·rufen auf·stehen an·kommen ein·laden mit·bringen an|fangen an|rufen auf|stehen an|kommen ein|laden mit|bringen E.2

## F. Past tense: was and had
F. Past tense: was and had So far the verbs you’ve been working with have been in the present tense — with the exception of two verbs that appeared at the end of Interaktion 3.20 : Annika: Wo warst du denn? Where were you? Jonas: Ich hatte keine Lust zu fahren. I had no desire to drive. The similarity between war and were , and between hatte and had , reflects the close relationship between the past tense in German and English, for these and many other verbs. Past tense: sein singular plural first-person ich war wir waren second-person du warst [familiar] Sie waren [formal] ihr wart [familiar] Sie waren [formal] third-person er/der Mann war sie/die Frau war es/das Kind war sie/die Leute waren Past tense: haben singular plural first-person ich hatte wir hatten second-person du hattest [familiar] Sie hatten [formal] ihr hattet [familiar] Sie hatten [formal] third-person er/der Mann hatte sie/die Frau hatte es/das Kind hatte sie/die Leute hatten While some of these forms look familiar ( -st with du ; normal endings for the plural forms), notice that the ich and er/sie/es forms are identical, and that the er/sie/es forms don't carry the usual -t ending. F

## G. Review: kein-/nicht
G. Review: kein-/nicht In Chapter 2 you learned two rules for saying what someone or something is not , in the nominative case: 1. Noun phrases beginning with ein/eine — or with no preceding article — are negated with kein/keine, meaning not a / no: Was für eine Kirche ist das? — Das ist keine Kirche, das ist eine Moschee. What kind of a church is that? — That’s not a church, that’s a mosque. Ist Bild am Sonntag eine gute Zeitung ? — Naja, viele Leute lesen sie, aber ich finde, das ist keine gute Zeitung. Is Bild am Sonntag a good newspaper? — Well, a lot of people read it, but in my opinion it’s not a good newspaper. Was für Musik ist das? — Das ist keine Musik, das ist Lärm! What kind of music is that? — That’s not music, that’s noise! This use of kein/keine applies to singular and plural sentence subjects that would be preceded in English by No … : Kein Problem ist zu groß für sie! No problem is too big for them! Keine Leute arbeiten hier am Sonntag? Echt?? No people work here on Sunday? Really?? 2. Otherwise you negate the information by placing nicht immediately before it: Heißt er Lukas ? — Nein, er heißt nicht Lukas , er heißt Jonas. Is his name Lukas? — No, his name isn’t Lukas, his name is Jonas. Wird die Situation langsam besser ? — Sie wird leider nicht besser . Is the situation slowly getting better? — Unfortunately, it's not getting better. Bist du heute gestresst ? — Nein, heute bin ich nicht gestresst. Are you stressed out today? — No, today I’m not stressed out. Sind die Geschäfte jetzt offen ? — Nein, es ist Sonntag, die Geschäfte sind nicht offen . Are the stores open now? — No, it’s Sunday, the stores aren’t open. G

## H. Negation: Verbs
H. Negation: Verbs So much for negating nouns and adjectives. Now: How do you negate verbs? German usage varies from English in this regard, and it’s important to be aware of the differences. To that end, think about how you would make the following statements in English negative, as in the example: Example: I give up. / I’m giving up. → I don’t give up. / I’m not giving up. He goes out. / He is going out. → She reads. / She is reading. → I work. / I am working. → We go running. / We’re going running. → Owing to the multiple aspects available in English (We run / We do run / We are running), English verbs require a fairly complex manipulation of doesn’t / don’t / isn’t / are not and other elements to formulate the negation. German verbs, on the other hand, require just one word: nicht You may have noticed in the examples above that English places the negating element before the verb. But if you want to negate just the verb in a German sentence — that is, if you want to say that someone is not doing something — you place nicht after the conjugated verb: Sie liest. → Sie liest nicht . Ich arbeite. → Ich arbeite nicht . Er geht aus. → Er geht nicht aus. Wir gehen laufen. → Wir gehen nicht laufen. Granted, most sentences aren’t as short as this, but the principle here is very important: If it’s just the verb that’s being negated, nicht appears after it. H
