## A. Review: Strong verbs I
A. Review: Strong verbs I If you want to talk in any detail about things that happened in the past, you need to know a wide range of verbs and their past-tense forms, especially their past participles. Recycling and reviewing all of the past participles you’ve learned so far might be helpful — but it would certainly also be tedious. A more efficient approach would be to focus on the strong verbs you’ve learned so far, since familiarity with these would allow you to assume that any other verbs are weak, and therefore predictable in their past tense formation. It's one strategy. But does it make sense if your active vocabulary consists of only 1,200 words? In fact, it does. German reference grammars typically list the principal parts (infinitive + simple past + past participle) of approximately 150 strong verbs. Of these, 123 are included in the core vocabulary of der|die|das. In other words, an astonishing 82% of the strong verbs in the entire German language are among the 1,200 most frequently used words — which means that knowing them well will allow you to make intelligent guesses about which verbs are weak or strong. To this end, the 100 or so strong verbs you’ve learned up to this point are reviewed in this section, with exercises and tasks to help you consolidate your knowledge of their meanings, use, and past participles forms. ## C. The passive voice
C. The passive voice C.1 When is a sentence “passive”? Depending on how much grammar you’re familiar with, you may or may not know the difference between “active” and “passive” sentences. “Passive” in this context has nothing to do with lethargy or lack of initiative. Rather, it means that a sentence focuses on what is happening and to whom or what , rather than on who or what is making it happen. Compare these two sentences, for example: (1) Following WWII, the Allied Powers divided Berlin into four sectors. (2) Following WWII, Berlin was divided into four sectors by the Allied Powers. In (1), the emphasis is on who did it and on what they did . In (2), the focus is on what happened, and only secondarily on who did it. In terms of grammar, (1) is “active” (i.e., in the “active voice”), whereas (2) is “passive” (i.e., in the “passive voice”). The difference is not in the information, but in the focus of attention. You may have heard in writing classes that you should avoid the passive voice. Don’t believe it. There are many situations in which emphasis is properly placed on what is happening (or has happened) rather than on who is (or was) responsible for doing it. A few examples: The government has been repeatedly criticized for … (the origin of the criticism isn’t the issue, but rather the criticism itself) Security forces were put on high alert yesterday when … (who put them on high alert doesn’t really matter) Congratulations! Your order has been shipped. (who took care of the shipping is irrelevant) Many German cities were destroyed by bombs … (it’s the destruction, not the bombs, that is important here) Your request is currently being processed. (by AI? by a bored and underpaid staff worker? who knows?) C.1
C.2 Forming passive constructions As you can see from C.1 and Übung 13.8 , passive constructions in English consist of a form of the verb to be — in various tenses and combined with modal verbs — and a past participle. to be Past participle was hired are conducted [should] be notified has been said [will] be closed [must] be removed were destroyed [can] be picked (up) is being processed [will] be accepted Taking accepted as an example, consider the ways it might appear in the passive voice: She has been accepted into a top grad school. They hope to be accepted. I just found out I was accepted! We were accepted last year too. He must not have been accepted. I know several applicants who should have been accepted. Notice how to be goes through all the changes required by different subjects, tenses and modal verbs, while accepted remains constant. C.2

## D. Passive: Present tense
D. Passive: Present tense D.1 If you understand how the passive voice is constructed in English, you should have no difficulties forming it in German. In place of the auxiliary verb to be , German uses werden ; and instead of grouping the auxiliary and the past participle together to form one phrase, German uses the same verbal bracket you've seen with modal verbs and the Perfekt tense, with the past participle functioning as V 2 . That’s it. By using werden , German emphasizes the idea of becoming — something is becoming done or has become done, or will become done (or built, or confirmed, or whatever V 2 happens to be). A few examples in the present tense: Ein neues Gebäude wird gerade auf dem Campus gebaut . A new building is being built right now on the campus. Ungefähr 2.000 Bewerber werden jedes Jahr aufgenommen . Approximately 2,000 applicants are accepted every year. In dependent clauses, werden (V 1 ) moves to the end: Weißt du, ob der Kurs dieses Semester angeboten wird ? Do you know if the course is (being) offered this semester? D.1
D.2 Depending on whether the speaker wants to emphasize the ongoing process (is being built) or the result of a process (is built) , English uses is being/are being or simply is/are as auxiliaries to form a passive verb in the present tense. In German there is only one option for this — the present tense of werden . For many English speakers, the connection between is being built and wird … gebaut isn't immediately clear, since is and being themselves don’t correlate directly with wird . (One more example of why words must always be understood in context, rather than merely as individual elements.) The exercises that follow are intended to help you understand and develop a feel for this connection, but in addition you should watch for examples of the passive voice and think about their form and meaning: Find headlines or captions in online media and consider how they would appear in English. Create passive sentences in German and picture their English equivalents (and vice versa). Create passive sentences in English, using words you know from the core vocabulary of this program, and translate them into German. D.2

## E. Passive: Simple past tense
E. Passive: Simple past tense E.1 To express a passive sentence in the past tense, German uses the same format as the present-tense passive, but substitutes wurden (Simple Past) for werden (Present) as the V 1 element. You can see this structure in play in many of the texts you've read in this chapter. Take for example this sentence from Interaktion 13.5: Das Lied wurde extra für eine Werbekampagne der »Berliner Morgenpost« komponiert und getextet. Reducing it to its verb components: wurde ... komponiert was composed wurde ... getextet was texted/written Here are a few more examples, showing how V 1 conjugates with the sentence subject, while the past participle ( V 2 ) remains unchanged: Unser Angebot wurde abgelehnt . Our offer was rejected. Ich wurde erst später über den Fall informiert . I wasn’t informed about the case until later. An wen wurden die E-Mails geschickt? To whom were the emails sent? Dieses Hotel wurde uns von Freunden empfohlen. This hotel was recommended to us by friends. Warum wurdest du nicht eingeladen? Why weren’t you invited? The glosses above illustrate how English tends to use the neutral auxiliary was rather than the progressive auxiliary was being for past-tense passives. But past-tense passives still convey a sense of having gone through the process of […], regardless of which auxiliary is used. E.1
E.2 As a review, here are the Simple Past forms of werden , combined with the past participle of einladen ( to invite ) to form a sample phrase: … was/were invited: singular plural ich wurde … eingeladen wir wurden … eingeladen du wurdest … eingeladen Sie wurden … eingeladen ihr wurdet … eingeladen Sie wurden … eingeladen er/der Mann wurde … eingeladen sie/die Frau wurde … eingeladen es/das Kind wurde … eingeladen sie/die Leute wurden … eingeladen E.2

## F. Passive: by / by means of / with
F. Passive: by / by means of / with F.1 Passive sentences don’t necessarily have to mention an “agent,” i.e., who or what caused the activity to happen, and in fact the passive voice is often used precisely because the agent is irrelevant or not known. But if the writer/speaker wishes to provide this information, a prepositional phrase is used beginning with von or durch . Both words can be translated as by in this context, but there’s an important distinction: von by (usually a human agent) durch by means of (usually a non-human agent) Compare: Der Berliner Dom wurde von Julius Raschdorff entworfen. The Berlin Cathedral was designed by Julius Raschdorff . Im Zweiten Weltkrieg wurde der Dom durch Bomben schwer beschädigt. In WWII, the cathedral was heavily damaged by (means of) bombs. Die Entscheidung wird vom ganzen Team getroffen. The decision is being made by the whole team. Sie werden durch eine E-Mail darüber informiert. You will be informed about it by (means of) an email. F.1
F.2 German also uses mit to describe the means by which something is done, but usually in contexts where some kind of instrument is in play, much like English uses with or by using or by means of: »Hamlet« wurde wohl nicht mit einem Laptop geschrieben . "Hamlet" was probably not written with a laptop computer. Fußball wird mit einem runden Ball gespielt . Soccer is played with (i.e., by using) a round ball. Das Konzert wurde mit drei kleinen Mikrofonen aufgenommen . The concert was recorded with (i.e., by using, by means of) three small microphones. F.2

## G. Passive: Modal verbs
G. Passive: Modal verbs G.1 Modal verbs are often used in passive constructions, both in English and in German. In English they look like this: More medications must be developed in light of the pandemic. The application is supposed to be submitted by noon today. The victim’s identity could not be confirmed . Notice that each sentence contains a similar phrase: the word be followed immediately by a past participle (be developed / be submitted / be confirmed) . This phrase is called a passive infinitive, and it functions like any other infinitive in a clause with a modal verb: Active: Someone must develop more medications … Passive: More medications must be developed … Active: Someone is supposed to submit the application… Passive: The application is supposed to be submitted … Active: They could not confirm the victim’s identity. Passive: The victim’s identity could not be confirmed . Passive infinitives in German are likewise formed with the past participle, but with werden placed after it in a fixed phrase: entwickelt werden be developed abgegeben werden be submitted bestätigt werden be confirmed G.1
G.2 Passive infinitives function just like the active infinitives you've been using all along. In clauses with modal verbs, V 1 and V 2 form the usual verbal bracket, the only difference being that the V 2 element consists of two words, rather than one: Noch mehr Medikamente müssen angesichts der Pandemie entwickelt werden. More medications need to be developed in light of the pandemic. Die Bewerbung soll heute bis 12 Uhr mittags abgegeben werden. The application is supposed to be submitted by noon today. Die Identität des Opfers konnte nicht bestätigt werden. The victim’s identity could not be confirmed. As always with V 1 - V 2 structures, changes in number and tense are reflected in the modal verb itself (V 1 ) , while the passive infinitive (V 2 ) remains unchanged. Notice how this plays out in the following examples: Es musste sofort gemacht werden! It had to be done immediately! Sie dürfen nicht gestört werden. They are not allowed to be disturbed. Das Programm soll später verändert werden. The program is supposed to be changed later. Sie kann leider nicht erreicht werden. Unfortunately, she cannot be reached. ausgenutzt° zerstört° G.2
G.3 Remember that V 1 verbs move to the end of dependent clauses. In clauses with a modal verb and a passive infinitive, this means that three verb forms will be lined up at the end. In terms of grammar, however, there are still only two elements: the passive infinitive ( V 2 ) and the conjugated verb ( V 1 ). Mir war schon immer klar, dass es sofort gemacht werden musste . It was always clear to me that it had to be done immediately. Weiß jemand, warum sie nicht erreicht werden kann ? Does anyone know why she cannot be reached? G.3

## H. Passive: Future tense / Perfect tense
H. Passive: Future tense / Perfect tense H.1 Passives in the Future tense Once you know how to use passive infinitives, expressing the passive voice in the future tense (e.g., it will be decided ) is relatively simple. As you learned in Chapter 12, the future tense is formed in the active voice by combining werden ( V 1 ) with an infinitive ( V 2 ). A few examples: 2000: Berlin wird einen neuen Flughafen haben ! 2000: Berlin will have a new airport! 2005: Die Bauarbeiten werden nächstes Jahr beginnen ! 2005: Construction will begin next year! 2012: Man wird dieses Jahr den neuen Flughafen eröffnen ! 2012: They’re going to open the new airport this year! 2013: Man wird spätestens nächstes Jahr den Flughafen eröffnen . 2013: They’re going to open the airport next year at the very latest. 2014: Man wird vielleicht 2017 den Flughafen eröffnen ... 2014: Maybe they will open the airport in 2017… 2019: Naja, hoffentlich wird man 2020 den Flughafen endlich eröffnen ... 2019: Well, one hopes they'll finally open the airport in 2020… Future tense constructions in the passive voice follow the same format, using werden plus an infinitive — but in this case it’s a passive infinitive: Der Flughafen wird in den nächsten Jahren eröffnet werden. The airport will be opened in the next few years. Die Eröffnung wird bestimmt groß gefeiert werden. The opening will definitely be celebrated in a big way. Aber die Verspätung wird wohl so schnell nicht vergessen werden. But the delay will probably not be forgotten soon. This can be confusing at first: werden appears twice, and it doesn’t take its basic meaning of to become in either appearance. Keep in mind: Paired with an infinitive, werden means will … , i.e., the future tense. Paired with a passive infinitive, which includes werden as well, it means will be [done] or going to be [done]. It’s a reminder, if you need one, that meanings often emerge from combinations of words in a particular context, rather than from one-to-one correspondences. H.1
H.2 Passives in the Perfect tense The passive voice is also used to talk about something that has been done : Our course of action has already been decided. Don’t worry — the problem has been solved. These two economic models have often been compared with each other. Have the tires on this car been changed recently? While these sentences refer to the past, they do so in a way that links what has been done to the present, as attested by the adverbs already and recently . German expresses this nuance with a passive formation in the Perfect tense, using a conjugated form of sein as V 1 and a special form of werden as V 2 : has been decided ist … entschieden worden has been solved ist … gelöst worden have been compared sind … verglichen worden have been changed sind … gewechselt worden What you see here is the Perfect tense of werden (i.e., ist … geworden ), but with geworden shortened to worden . This form occurs only in the Perfect tenses of the passive voice, which makes these passive constructions easy to identify. Der Plan ist erfolgreich durchgeführt worden. The plan has been carried out successfully. Es ist schon festgestellt worden , dass ... It’s already been determined that … Bisher sind zwei Spieler verletzt worden . Up to now, two players have been injured. Full disclosure #1: Das ist gemacht worden translates equally well two ways: That has been done (Perfect tense) as well as That was done (Simple Past). Likewise, Das wurde gemacht can mean That has been done , but more often carries the meaning That was done. For now, if you want to say has been [done], you should use the Perfect passive tense. Full disclosure #2: There is also a Past Perfect tense in the passive voice, which is formed with war/waren + a past participle + worden , and translates as had been [done]. This construction is used so rarely that it’s not necessary at this point to focus on it. H.2
