## B. Past Perfect
B. Past Perfect We begin with the Past Perfect and move through the tenses indicated in the graphic — though intentionally skipping the Perfect and Present tenses, both of which should be very familiar to you by now. As the graphic indicates, the Past Perfect locates an activity prior to another activity that takes place the past (relative to the “present” of the speaker). Wir hatten schon gegessen, als ... We had already eaten when ... Er war gerade angekommen, als ... He had just arrived when ... Structurally, the Past Perfect is identical to the Perfect tense, using a V 1 — V 2 structure with conjugated forms of war or hatte (the Simple Past forms of sein and haben ) serving as a V 1 auxiliary. The choice of war or hatte as the V 1 depends on whether the V 2 verb takes sein or haben in the Perfect tense. In a main clause, V 1 is the second element, with V 2 at the end: Im ersten Semester war er zu jeder Vorlesung gegangen, aber dann ... During the first semester he had gone to every lecturer, but then ... Leider hatte sie seinen Geburtstag nochmal vergessen . Unfortunately she had once again forgotten his birthday. In a dependent clause, the V 1 auxiliary moves to the end: ..., obwohl sie ihm eine SMS geschickt hatte. ..., although she had sent him a text message. ..., nachdem es zweimal passiert war . ..., after it had happened twice.

## C. Simple Past
C. Simple Past Like the Perfect tense, the Simple Past is used when referring to events, actions or states that took place in the past and are no longer occurring*. But the Simple Past differs from the Perfect tense in function and form. Function: The Simple Past is used predominantly in narrative texts (novels, short stories, anecdotes) to convey a sense of moving through narrative time. Conversational references to the past (“How was your weekend?”) are normally expressed in the Perfect tense, with the exception of frequently used verbs such as sein, haben and the modal verbs, all of which consistently use the Simple Past for past-time references. Form: Unlike the Perfect tense, the Simple Past consists of only one word, formed in one of three ways, depending on whether the verb is a weak [“t”] verb, a strong [“n”] verb, or a “mixed” verb. C.1 Simple Past: Weak [“t”] verbs Weak [“t”] verbs have stems that remain constant in all tenses, and past participles that end with a -t . Weak verbs form the Simple Past tense by adding a -t to the verb stem (or -et if the stem already ends with a -t ), and the following endings: singular plural first-person ich -e wir -en second-person du ( familiar ) Sie ( formal ) -est -en ihr ( familiar ) Sie ( formal ) -et -en third-person er/sie/es -e sie -en A few examples: sagen : sag + t + e → Er sagte das. antworten : antwort + et + en → Die Kinder antworteten sofort. liefern : liefer + t + e → Die Firma lieferte das Paket. Separable-prefix verbs follow the same pattern with respect to the main verb: einrichten : richt + et + en → Wir richteten unser Wohnzimmer ein . You can access a complete list of all the weak verbs in chapters 1-16, showing a default conjugation of third-person singular ( er/sie/es ), by clicking here . * Remember: Even if it began in the past, an activity that is ongoing in the speaker's “present” is expressed by using the Present tense. You can refer either to the point at which it started or its duration by using the dative preposition seit : We have been learning German (implied: and we still are) since August. → Wir lernen Deutsch seit August. / We have been learning German for nine months. → Wir lernen Deutsch seit neun Monaten.
C.2 Simple Past: Strong verbs Strong [“n”] verbs represent a relatively small percentage of all German verbs — there are only about 125 or so in total, depending on which list you consult — but a remarkably high percentage of these (65%) are among the 1,200 most frequently used words in German. (By way of comparison: There are literally thousands of weak [“t”] verbs, of which only 168 appear in the core vocabulary list.) The Simple Past stem of strong verbs shows a different vowel (or vowels) than the infinitive stem, and in some cases different consonants as well. First-person ( ich ) and third-person ( er/sie/es ) conjugations of strong verbs in the Simple Past take no endings, much like modal verbs in the present tense. The other conjugations are likewise similar to modal verbs: singular plural first-person ich [no ending] wir -en second-person du ( familiar ) Sie ( formal ) -st -en ihr ( familiar ) Sie ( formal ) -t -en third-person er/sie/es [no ending] sie -en A few examples: verschwinden : Als sie verschwand ... When she disappeared, ... fahren : Sie fuhren viel zu schnell. They drove much too fast. stoßen : Seine Idee stieß auf scharfe Kritik. His idea ran into sharp criticism. You can access a complete list of all the strong verbs in chapters 1-16, showing the default third-person singular conjugation ( er/sie/es ) — which is identical to the Simple Past stem — by clicking here .
C.3 Simple Past: Mixed verbs “Mixed” verbs show characteristics of both weak and strong verbs. Their Simple Past forms incorporate a -t- and take the Simple Past endings used with weak verbs; but they also show the stem-vowel change that is characteristic of strong verbs. Except for sollen and wollen (which show no vowel change from Present to Simple Past), the Simple Past forms for modal verbs are “mixed”: dürfen können mögen müssen sollen wollen Simple Past stem: durf t- konn t- moch t- muss t- soll t- woll t- ich durfte konnte mochte musste sollte wollte (familiar) du (formal) Sie durftest durftet konntest konnten mochtest mochten musstest mussten solltest sollten wolltest wollten er/sie/es durfte konnte mochte musste sollte wollte wir durften konnten mochten mussten sollten wollten (familiar) ihr (formal) Sie durftet durften konntet konnten mochtet mochten musstet mussten solltet sollten wolltet wollten sie durften konnten mochten mussten sollten wollten The following verbs from the core vocabulary follow the same pattern, with a changed stem ending in -t , and the Simple Past endings for weak verbs. infinitive stem example bringen brach t- Sie brachte ein Buch mit. denken dach t- Er dachte oft an den vergangenen Sommer. erkennen erkann t- Sie erkannte seine Stimme. kennen kann t- Er kannte nur ein paar Leute da. nennen nann t- Sie nannten den alten Wagen »The Green Dragon«. wissen wuss t- Das wusste ich gar nicht! Haben and werden are also considered mixed verbs. Haben changes its final stem consonant from -b to -tt and adds the weak verb Simple Past endings. Werden acts more like a strong verb than the others in this category, in that its stem shows a vowel change while still retaining the -d of the infinitive; but like other mixed verbs, it takes the Simple Past endings used with weak verbs. haben Simple Past stem: ha tt- ich hatte (familiar) du (formal) Sie hattest hatten er/sie/es hatte wir hatten (familiar) ihr (formal) Sie hattet hatten sie hatten werden Simple Past stem: wur d- ich wurde (familiar) du (formal) Sie wurdest wurden er/sie/es wurde wir wurden (familiar) ihr (formal) Sie wurdet wurden sie wurden

## D. Future Tense
D. Future Tense Forms German expresses the Future tense (I will ... / I'm going to ...) by combining a present-tense form of werden as V 1 with a V 2 infinitive. In independent clause statements and Wer-wie-was questions, werden is the second element; in Verb-first questions it appears first. Sie werden schon sehen ! Wann werden wir endlich anfangen ? Wirst du noch in 20 Jahren an mich denken ? In dependent clauses, werden moves to the end of the clause, following the V 2 infinitive: Ich weiß gar nicht, was ich machen werde . Modal verbs can be expressed in the Future tense (will be able to ... / will have to ...) by using werden as V 1 , and moving the modal verb itself — as an infinitive — to the end of the clause. Du wirst das sicher machen können . You will definitely be able to do that. Use In practice, most German speakers refer to the future by using verbs in the Present tense along with future-pointing modifiers ( nächste Woche / später / in zwei Jahren ). The Future tense is normally reserved for contexts in which future reference isn't clear from the context, i.e., when using the Present tense would appear to signify only the present; when referring to a relatively distant future; or to emphasize that something really will happen. Compare German and English usage in sentences that contain an adverbial future reference: Wir machen das später. We'll do that later. / We're going to do that later. Ich lese es am Wochenende. I'll read it over the weekend. / I'm going to read it over the weekend. Machst du diesen Sommer ein Praktikum? Will you be doing ... / Are you going to do an internship this summer? But: Was machst du? What are you doing? Was wirst du machen ? What will you do? / What are you going to do?

## E. Imperative mood
E. Imperative mood Using the imperative mood in German (Please wait for me. / Speak more slowly! / Come back soon! / Run faster!) requires you to identify who it is you're speaking to, since German (unlike English) distinguishes in these forms between the number of addressees and levels of formality. When speaking to someone (or a group of people) whom you would address with Sie : [Infinitive] + Sie ... Warten Sie bitte auf mich! Sprechen Sie langsamer! Kommen Sie bald zurück! Laufen Sie schneller! When speaking to a group of people whom you would address with ihr : [ ihr -form of the verb] ... Wartet bitte auf mich! Sprecht langsamer! Kommt bald zurück! Lauft schneller! When speaking to someone whom you would address with du : [ du -form of the verb, minus the -st ending] ... Warte bitte auf mich! Sprich langsamer! Komm(e) bald zurück! Lauf(e) schneller! Additional guidelines for the du -imperative: If the du -form shows a stem-vowel change from e → ie , the du -imperative retains this change: Sprich ... ! If the du -form shows a stem-vowel change from a → ä , the du -imperative does NOT retain this change: Lauf ... ! An -e is sometimes added to the du -imperative. In most cases this is optional, but there are two contexts for which there are rules: • If the du -form shows a stem-vowel change, no -e can be added: Sprich ...! • If the du -form ends in -d or -t , an -e MUST be added: Warte ...! When speaking to a group that includes yourself, as in Let's go!: [Infinitive] + wir ... Warten wir noch ein paar Minuten. Sprechen wir darüber. Laufen wir bitte nicht so schnell! For general directives, i.e., with no specific addressee in mind: _________ [Infinitive]! Vor dem Halt nicht aufstehen! No standing up before the stop! Kein Englisch sprechen! No speaking English! An der Kasse zahlen! Pay at the counter! NB: In all but the final category, separable-prefix verbs split, with the prefix moving to the end of the clause. Also: Notice that while exclamation points are often used with verbs in the imperative mood, they are not strictly necessary. The imperative forms for sein are irregular: Sie: Seien Sie ... ! ihr: Seid ... ! du: Sei ... ! wir: Seien wir ... !

## F. Subjunctive
F. Subjunctive The Subjunctive mood is used in German (and English): to express contrary-to-fact hypotheses about the past or present: If only you had been here, ... If I weren't sick at the moment, ... If we had a test today, ... to express wishes and speculations that are hypothetical, but potentially possible: It would be so great if she could help us ... Maybe you could come for a visit ... If only there were some way that we could ... to express polite requests and comments: Would you please help me? We would absolutely love to go! Could you close the window? F.1 Present Subjunctive Theoretically, at least, Present Subjunctive forms exist for all verbs, but in practice only a few are used, as discussed in Thema 1 of Chapter 15. For this review, we'll highlight the forms you'll encounter most often. (You can refer to the relevant sections of Chapter 15 for more details.) 1. The Big Three Far and away the most frequently used Present Subjunctive forms are those for the verbs werden, sein and haben — so be sure you are very familiar with the following forms and their meanings: würde would subjunctive stem: würd- ich würde (familiar) du (formal) Sie würdest würden er/sie/es würde wir würden (familiar) ihr (formal) Sie würdet würden sie würden wäre would be subjunctive stem: wär- ich wäre (familiar) du (formal) Sie wärest wären er/sie/es wäre wir wären (familiar) ihr (formal) Sie wäret wären sie wären hätte would have subjunctive stem: hätt- ich hätte (familiar) du (formal) Sie hättest hätten er/sie/es hätte wir hätten (familiar) ihr (formal) Sie hättet hätten sie hätten A few examples: Es wäre schön, wenn du mitkommen würdest . It would be nice if you'd come along. Glaub mir, ich würde es machen, wenn ich Zeit hätte . Believe me, I would do it if I had time. 2. Modal/mixed verbs Modal verbs — particularly können and mögen — and mixed verbs such as wissen appear frequently in the Present Subjunctive: könnte could subjunctive stem: könnt- ich könnte (familiar) du (formal) Sie könntest könnten er/sie/es könnte wir könnten (familiar) ihr (formal) Sie könntet könnten sie könnten möchte would like (to) subjunctive stem: möcht- ich möchte (familiar) du (formal) Sie möchtest möchten er/sie/es möchte wir möchten (familiar) ihr (formal) Sie möchtet möchten sie möchten wüsste knew subjunctive stem: wüsst- ich wüsste (familiar) du (formal) Sie wüsstest wüssten er/sie/es wüsste wir wüssten (familiar) ihr (formal) Sie wüsstet wüssten sie wüssten Examples: Ich könnte vielleicht ein Praktikum finden. Maybe I could find an internship. Wir möchten einen Tisch am Fenster. We would like a table at the window. Wenn du wüsstest , wie sehr ich dich liebe , ... If you knew how much I love you, ... 3. Würde + infinitive For almost all other verbs, the preferred way of expressing the Present Subjunctive is with würde (would) + infinitive : Ich würde dir gern helfen , aber ... I would love to help you, but ... So eine Maßnahme würde die Wirtschaft bestimmt verbessern , oder? A measure like that would definitely improve the economy, right?
F.2 Past Subjunctive / Past Modal Subjunctive The Past Subjunctive refers to what might have been in the past, as a contrary-to-fact hypothesis or wish. In English, the Past Subjunctive is formed with the auxiliary had or would have and a past participle: If only I had known , ... I would have known what to do if ... German also forms the Past Subjunctive with an auxiliary ( V 1 ) and a past participle ( V 2 ), but the auxiliary is a conjugated form of hätte or wäre , depending on whether the main verb takes haben or sein as its auxiliary for the Perfect tense. Examples with verbs taking haben : Ich hätte wohl gefragt, ob ... I probably would have asked if ... Das hätte gar keinen Spaß gemacht. That would not have been fun at all. Du hättest genauso reagiert. You would have reacted exactly the same way. Examples with verbs taking sein : Du wärest überrascht gewesen. You would have been surprised. Es wäre nie passiert, wenn ... It would never have happened if ... Wenn Sie mitgekommen wären , ... If you had come along, ... To express I should have ... / I could have ... , German uses a conjugated form of hätte as V 1 , and places sollen (should have) or können (could have) at the very end of the clause, after the infinitive associated with the modal verb. Clauses with this construction end with two infinitives, back to back: Ich hätte deine E-Mail lesen sollen . I should have read your email. Du hättest mich fragen können ! You could have asked me! Wir hätten zu Hause bleiben sollen . We should have stayed home.

## G. Passive voice
G. Passive voice G.1 Passive: Present / Simple Past The Passive voice focuses attention on an activity, rather than on who or what is performing that activity, and is formed in German with werden as V 1 and a past participle as V 2 . Das neue Wohnheim wird endlich gebaut . The new dorm is finally being built. Der Fall wurde sofort untersucht . The case was investigated immediately. Points to keep in mind: The Passive voice can be expressed in all tenses by using the corresponding forms of werden (reviewed in the sections below). The past participle in a Passive sentence remains unchanged and in the V 2 position. The “agent” in a Passive sentence — the person or entity responsible for the activity — can be indicated with a prepositional phrase beginning with von (by) or durch (by means of) . While the Passive voice can be expressed in any tense, it is most often found in the Present and the Simple Past tenses, which are shown below. Its use in the Perfect and Future tenses, and with modal verbs, is reviewed in G.2 . The present-tense Passive — indicating that something is being done — is constructed with present-tense forms of werden : Ich werde oft gefragt, ... I'm often asked ... Wirst du oft gefragt, ...? Are you often asked ...? Sie wird nur manchmal gefragt, ... She is only sometimes asked ... Wir werden nie gefragt, ... We're never asked ... Werdet ihr manchmal gefragt, ...? Are you (all) asked sometimes ...? So weit ich weiß, werden sie kaum gefragt, ... As far as I know, they are hardly ever asked ... The Simple Past Passive — indicating that something was (being) done — is expressed using Simple Past forms of wurden : Ich wurde informiert. I was informed. Wurdest du informiert? Were you informed? Er wurde nicht informiert. He wasn't informed. Wir wurden später informiert, dass ... We were informed later that ... Wurdet ihr darüber informiert? Were you (all) informed about it? Sie wurden leider nicht informiert. Unfortunately, they weren't informed.
G.2 Passive: Perfect / Future / Modal verbs A Passive sentence in the Perfect tense is constructed with werden in the Perfect tense — but with a twist. Rather than the expected ist ... geworden , Passive sentences use a shortened form of the past participle: ist ... worden : Das ist schon gemacht worden . That has already been done. / That was already done. Die E-Mails sind geschickt worden . The emails have been sent. / The emails were sent. As the English glosses indicate, the Perfect Passive can mean has been done or was (being) done , but the first meaning is more closely tied to the Perfect tense, and the latter to the Simple Past. A few more examples: Der Plan ist geändert worden . The plan has been changed. Sind die Reservierungen bestätigt worden ? Have the reservations been confirmed? Du bist ersetzt worden . Tut mir leid. You've been replaced. Sorry. The Future Passive follows the usual format of pairing werden ( V 1 ) with an infinitive ( V 2 ). But in this case, V 2 is a passive infinitive, consisting of a past participle + werden : diskutiert werden be discussed vermieden werden be avoided erwähnt werden be mentioned Using these examples in full sentences: Der Plan wird bestimmt diskutiert werden . The plan will definitely be discussed. Sie werden wohl vermieden werden . They will probably be avoided. Wird das erwähnt werden ? Will that be mentioned? Modal verbs can also be paired with passive infinitives: Der Plan muss heute diskutiert werden . The plan must be discussed today. Das darf nicht vermieden werden . That must not be avoided. Kann das später erwähnt werden ? Can that be mentioned later? The modal verb itself can take on various tenses and moods, but the passive infinitive remains constant. Der Plan musste gestern diskutiert werden . The plan had to be discussed yesterday. Der Plan müsste schnell diskutiert werden . The plan would have to be discussed soon.

## I. Comparative + Superlative
I. Comparative + Superlative German frequently uses the same word as an adjective (to modify a noun) and an adverb (to modify a verb, and adjective or another adverb). For the review below, we’ll use “modifier” to refer to both. You can access a list of all the modifiers in chapters 1-16 that take comparative/superlative forms by clicking here . I.1 Comparative + Superlative: Forms / Use Suffixes for comparative and superlative modifiers: Comparative: add -er to the base (“positive”) form of the modifier: schön → schöner Superlative: add -st to the base form: schön → schönst- Add -est as the superlative suffix: when the modifier ends with -t : härtest- when the modifier ends with an -s sound: heißest- / kürzest- when the modifier ends with a vowel: neuest- Remember: English has two ways of forming comparatives and superlatives. Short modifiers take suffixes ( -er / -est ), but longer ones require phrases ( more ____, most ____ ). German uses only suffixes, regardless of the modifier’s length. It’s tempting for English speakers to use mehr when English requires more, as in more beautiful, more difficult or more intelligent, but in all such cases German uses just the suffix: schöner / schwieriger / intelligenter. Adjectives that precede nouns always take endings, and the ending itself depends on the grammatical context, remaining the same for positive, comparative and superlative adjectives: eine praktisch e Idee eine praktisch er e Idee die praktisch st e Idee ein wichtig es Gesetz ein wichtig er es Gesetz das wichtig st e Gesetz Comparisons with so ... wie ... and als make use of predicate adjectives, which do not take endings: so spät wie du as late as you (are) genauso intelligent wie er exactly as intelligent as he (is) zweimal so alt wie ich twice as old as I (am) glücklicher als je zuvor happier than ever before Superlative adverbs use the form am ... -sten: Wer spielt am besten ? Who plays the best? Du sprichst am leisesten . You speak the most softly. Superlative adjectives likewise use am ... -sten when they appear as predicate adjectives, i.e., at the end of a clause, after the noun that they modify: Mein Zimmer ist wohl am kleinsten . My room is probably the smallest (one). Von all meinen Kursen ist dieser am interessantesten . Of all my courses, this one is the most interesting. When they appear before the nouns they modify, however, superlative adjectives take whatever ending is required by the context, without am : Ich habe das kleinste Zimmer im ganzen Wohnheim. I have the smallest room in the whole dorm. Stimmt nicht. Ich wohne im kleinsten Zimmer. Wrong. I have the smallest room.

## J. Dependent clauses
J. Dependent clauses Dependent clauses are word groups that include a verb, and that attach to independent clauses to form complete sentences. German has three kinds of dependent clauses: infinitive clauses [Chapter 8] subordinate clauses [Chapter 10] relative clauses [Chapter 14] In all three types, the verb appears at the end of the clause — with infinitive clauses (as the name indicates) as an infinitive, and with the other two types as a conjugated verb [ V 1 ]. Subordinate clauses will be reviewed in section J, and relative clauses in section K. [For a review of infinitive clauses, go to C.1 in Thema 2 of Chapter 8.] J.1 Subordinating conjunctions Subordinate clauses begin with subordinating conjunctions, which link the information in the subordinate clause to the information in the independent clause. Here is the full list of subordinating conjunctions in the core vocabulary (1-16), including those that were introduced in Chapter 11 as well as several words that were introduced in other contexts but can also serve as subordinating conjunctions: als when [referring to the past]; as als ob as if [often used with subjunctive verbs] auch wenn even if bevor before bis until damit so that [i.e., intention] dass that indem by ____ing nachdem after obwoh l although seit since [time, not causation] während while weil because wenn when [referring to the present/future]; whenever A few examples, showing some distinctions in meanings and use: Es sind schon zwei Monate, seit das Semester begonnen hat. It's already been two months since the semester began. Er tut, als ob ihm alles egal wäre. He acts as if nothing mattered to him. Auch wenn man nicht will, ... Even if one doesn't want to, ... Sie trank Kaffee, damit sie wach bleiben konnte. She drank coffee so that (i.e., with the intention that) she could stay awake. Du kannst mir helfen, indem du alles erklärst. You can help me by explaining everything.
J.2 Als | wenn | wann | ob English uses when in a variety of situations, each of which requires a different word in German; German in turn uses wenn in situations where English distinguishes between when and if; and once you have that sorted out, keep in mind that if covers two meanings in English for which German uses two words. A brief review: When When the plane arrives, we should ... [Present tense] → Wenn ... When the plane arrived, ... [Past tense] → Als ... When is it arriving? [Question] → Wann ...? If / whether It will arrive late if ... [conditional] → wenn ... I just don't know if ... [whether] → ob ...
J.3 Indirect questions An indirect question is a question in the form of a dependent clause that is connected to a main clause. The dependent clause may express a direct question posed by someone else, as indirect discourse; or it may simply express an idea that could stand alone as a question. The resulting sentence can be either a statement or a question. Direct question: »Wann beginnt das Programm?« Indirect question: Er hat gefragt, wann das Programm beginnt. Auf der Webseite steht nicht, wann das Programm beginnt. Weiß jemand vielleicht, wann das Programm beginnt? Both “wer-wie-was” questions and “verb-first” questions can be framed as indirect questions, following the format of all dependent clauses, with V 1 at the end. A. Wer-wie-was “Wer-wie-was” questions can be re-framed as indirect questions by using the interrogative words as subordinating conjunctions. These include words such as was and warum , but also phrasal elements such as wie oft and wie viel ; prepositions followed by wen or wem ; and wo- compounds: wann warum was welch- wer/wen/wem wie wie viel(e) wo woher wohin wie [lang] [für, um, auf …] wen [mit, aus, auf … ] wem wo[rüber] Wir haben uns noch nicht entschieden, wohin wir in Urlaub fahren wollen. We haven't decided yet where we want to go on vacation. Weißt du, wie lang das Spiel dauert? Do you know long the game will last? Ich bin nicht sicher, auf wen wir warten. I'm not sure who(m) we're waiting for. B. Verb-first Indirect “verb-first” questions can be re-framed as dependent clauses by using ob as the subordinating conjunction: Direct question: »Will sie mitkommen?« Indirect question: Sie kann sich nicht entscheiden, ob sie mitkommen will. A few more examples: Ich weiß nicht, ob mir der Film gefällt oder nicht. I don't know if I like the film or not. Frag ihn, ob es ein Starbucks in der Nähe gibt. Ask him if there's a Starbucks nearby.

## K. Relative clauses
K. Relative clauses A relative clause provides additional information about a sentence element (most often a noun) by way of a dependent clause: book → the book that I read last week . The sentence element being modified is often referred to as the antecedent , and the link between the antecedent and the modifying clause is called a relative pronoun . K.1 Relative pronouns 1. The choice of which relative pronoun to use is determined by two factors: the antecedent: masculine, feminine, neuter or plural the grammatical function of the relative pronoun within the relative clause: as a subject (Nominative): the book that was lying on the table as a direct (Accusative) or indirect (Dative) object: the book that I'm reading as the object of a preposition (Accusative/Dative; see K.2 below): the book about which we spoke in class expressing a relationship between the antecedent and another element (Genitive): a book whose author is not well known With respect to the table of relative pronouns below: The column is determined by the gender and number of the antecedent; the row is determined by the relative pronoun's function within its own clause — not by the case of the antecedent in its own clause. masculine feminine neuter plural Nominative der die das die Accusative den die das die Dative dem der dem denen Genitive dessen deren dessen deren 2. Using relative pronouns: A relative pronoun serving as the subject, direct object or indirect object of a dependent clause is always the first element in the clause. When a relative pronoun functions as the object of a preposition — the people with whom we spoke — the prepositional phrase is the first element in the clause, and cannot be split apart (as it can be in casual English: the people whom we spoke with ). Relative pronouns that function as objects of any kind within the relative clause cannot be dropped in German, as they often are in English (i.e., the people we spoke with ). As in all dependent clauses, the conjugated verb in the clause ( V 1 ) moves to the end: ... die Leute, mit denen wir gesprochen haben . 3. For non-gendered antecedents such as viel, etwas or nichts , the relative pronoun is was : Es gibt viel, was ich dir sagen muss. There's a lot (that) I need to tell you.
K.2 Relative pronouns + prepositions When a relative pronoun serves as the object of a preposition in the relative clause, the preposition/relative pronoun combination stands at the beginning of the clause; the conjugated verb ( V 1 ) moves to the end, as usual; and the relative pronoun takes the grammatical case required by the preposition — which in practice means a choice between Accusative and Dative relative pronouns: masculine feminine neuter plural Accusative den die das die Dative dem der dem denen ... der Freund, für den wir eine Abschiedsfeier organisiert haben ... the friend who we organized a going-away party for / for whom we organized a going-away party ... das Gebäude, vor dem sie steht ... the building [that] she's standing in front of ... ein paar Leute, mit denen ich sprechen möchte ... a few people I'd like to talk with / with whom I'd like to talk ... die SMS, auf die ich gewartet habe ... the text message [that] I was waiting for With non-gendered antecedents (e.g., viel, etwas, nichts ), the preposition/relative pronoun combination is replaced with a wo- compound: Gibt es etwas , worauf du dich besonders freust? Is there something [that] you're particularly looking forward to? Der Professor hat viel gesagt, woran ich mich gar nicht erinnern kann. The professor said a lot that I can't remember at all.
K.3 Relative pronouns + prepositions: A review The guidelines for using preposition/relative pronoun combinations in relative clauses are straightforward enough. But to pair a relative pronoun correctly with a preposition requires knowing which case that preposition takes. Here is a brief review: always Accusative, whether free-standing or in a fixed combination: durch für gegen ohne um ... die Adresse, um die du mich gebeten hast ... the address [that] you asked me for ... ein Kurs, für den ich mich echt interessiere ... a course I'm really interested in always Dative, whether free-standing or in a fixed combination: ab aus außer bei gegenüber mit nach seit von zu ... einige Freunde, mit denen wir ausgingen ... several friends we went out with / with whom we went out ... das Fußballspiel, von dem ich dir erzählte ... the soccer game I told you about variable: Accusative in contexts implying motion toward a goal; Dative in contexts implying position: an auf in hinter neben über unter vor zwischen ... das Zimmer, in dem ihre Sachen liegen ... the room in which her things are located ... das Zimmer, in das sie ihre Sachen trug ... the room she carried her things into / into which she carried her things variable: Accusative or Dative, depending on the case required in a fixed combination: Angst haben + vor [Dat.] arbeiten + an [Dat.] denken + an [Acc.] (sich) erinnern + an [Acc.] Erinnerung + an [Acc.] (sich) freuen + auf [Acc.] Hinweis + auf [Acc.] lachen + über [Acc.] reagieren + auf [Acc.] Reaktion + auf [Acc.] schreiben + über [Acc.] (sich) verlassen + auf [Acc.] verzichten + auf [Acc.] warten + auf [Acc.] ... das Experiment, an dem sie gerade arbeitet ... the experiment [that] she's working on right now / on which she's working right now ... Freunde, auf die ich mich verlassen kann ... friends [whom] I can rely on / on whom I can rely
